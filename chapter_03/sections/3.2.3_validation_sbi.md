## Validation of Simulation-Based Inference
\label{sec:3.2.3}

A neural density estimator trained on finite simulations can, in principle, produce posteriors that are poorly calibrated: the reported credible intervals may be systematically too narrow or too wide. Validating SBI results is therefore an integral part of any simulation-based analysis, and the simulator itself provides the means for doing so.

The standard validation framework is \emph{simulation-based calibration} (SBC), introduced by Talts et al.~\cite{Talts:2018}. The procedure is conceptually simple. One samples parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$ from the prior, generates synthetic data $\mathbf{x}^* \sim p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}^*)$ using the simulator, and then applies the trained estimator to obtain the approximate posterior $\hat{p}(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{x}^*)$. Because the true parameters are known by construction, one can check whether the posterior places the correct amount of probability mass around $\boldsymbol{\theta}^*$. Repeating this test across hundreds or thousands of independent simulations yields a statistical picture of the estimator's calibration: if the $(1-\alpha)$ credible intervals contain the true parameters in exactly a fraction $(1-\alpha)$ of trials, the estimator is well-calibrated. Systematic deviations indicate over- or underdispersion of the posterior~\cite{Cranmer:2020,Talts:2018}.

SBC is particularly well-suited to amortized estimators, since evaluating the posterior for each new synthetic observation is nearly free once the network has been trained. For non-amortized methods, the computational cost of fitting a new posterior for each of the hundreds of required test datasets would be prohibitive.

A complementary approach is \emph{coverage testing}: for a given confidence level, one directly measures the fraction of test cases in which the true parameters fall within the corresponding credible region. The empirical coverage can be compared to the nominal level across a range of credibility thresholds, and the discrepancy is formally quantified through the empirical calibration error~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. Departures from the diagonal in a coverage-vs-credibility plot flag potential miscalibration.

In practice, many SBI analyses supplement these general calibration checks with problem-specific diagnostics. In Paper~1~\cite{Amerio:2023dns}, for example, the internally estimated Bayesian uncertainties from the CNN are cross-checked against frequentist error bars derived from the distribution of residuals over the validation set. The close agreement between the two error estimates provides confidence in the reliability of the uncertainty quantification without requiring access to the true posterior --- which, for the $dN/dS$ problem, is unknown. The details of this validation strategy are presented in Chapter~6.

Taken together, SBC, coverage diagnostics, and problem-specific cross-checks form the validation toolkit that ensures simulation-based posteriors can be trusted for scientific inference. We will apply some combination of these diagnostics in each of the analyses presented in the subsequent chapters of this thesis.
