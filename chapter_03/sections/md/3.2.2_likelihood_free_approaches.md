## Likelihood-Free Approaches
\label{sec:3.2.2}

Given the impossibility of evaluating the likelihood directly, SBI replaces explicit likelihood computation with learned surrogates trained on simulated data. The idea has a long history: Diggle and Gratton~\cite{Diggle:1984} proposed using kernel density estimation to approximate intractable likelihoods from simulator output as early as 1984, while Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC) developed in parallel as an entirely sampling-based alternative~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. Both approaches, however, suffer from the curse of dimensionality: they require hand-crafted summary statistics to compress the data into low-dimensional representations, inevitably discarding information about the parameters of interest. ABC additionally lacks amortization, meaning the full inference procedure must be repeated for each new observation. Modern neural SBI methods overcome these limitations by using deep neural networks as flexible density estimators that operate directly on high-dimensional data~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. We now briefly describe the three main neural strategies that have emerged, distinguished by which statistical quantity their surrogate network learns to approximate.

\emph{Neural Posterior Estimation} (NPE) trains a conditional density estimator --- typically a normalizing flow --- to directly approximate the posterior distribution $p(\boldsymbol{\theta}|\mathbf{x})$~\cite{Greenberg:2019,Papamakarios:2021}. A normalizing flow maps samples from a simple base distribution (such as a multivariate Gaussian) through a sequence of parameterized, invertible transformations with tractable Jacobians, producing a flexible approximation to any target density~\cite{Papamakarios:2021}. By conditioning these transformations on the observed data $\mathbf{x}$, the flow learns to output the posterior density for any input observation. Because NPE directly provides the posterior, it is the most straightforward approach for Bayesian inference. However, because the prior enters the training, changing the prior requires retraining.

\emph{Neural Likelihood Estimation} (NLE) instead approximates the likelihood function $p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta})$, conditioning the density estimator on $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ rather than on $\mathbf{x}$~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. Once the likelihood surrogate is trained, Bayesian inference proceeds through standard MCMC sampling with any choice of prior. This separation of the likelihood from the prior provides additional flexibility, at the cost of an extra sampling step.

\emph{Neural Ratio Estimation} (NRE) takes a different approach altogether: rather than learning a probability density, a classifier is trained via supervised learning to discriminate between synthetic datasets generated at different parameter points. The classifier output can be converted into the likelihood ratio $p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_0)/p(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\theta}_1)$ --- a result closely related to the Neyman--Pearson lemma~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. Because the ratio is all that is needed for hypothesis testing and constructing confidence sets, NRE is particularly well-suited to frequentist applications.

All three strategies share a common advantage: \emph{amortized inference}. The computationally expensive steps --- running the simulator and training the neural network --- are performed once during an upfront training phase. After this phase, applying the trained surrogate to new observed data is nearly instantaneous~\cite{Cranmer:2020}. Furthermore, the neural network implicitly marginalizes over the vast latent space of the simulator, eliminating the need for hand-crafted summary statistics and preserving information that traditional methods would discard.

In this thesis, NPE is the SBI strategy most directly relevant to our work. In Paper~1~\cite{Amerio:2023dns}, a convolutional neural network is trained on 900{,}000 synthetic gamma-ray sky maps to directly estimate the $dN/dS$ and its uncertainty for any input map, without ever evaluating an explicit likelihood. The CNN outputs both a point estimate and a learned error bar for each flux bin, combining the roles of posterior mean and variance estimation. Once trained, the network can be applied to the real 14-year Fermi-LAT sky map to immediately obtain the source-count distribution --- a task that would require prohibitively expensive likelihood evaluations with traditional methods. The full details of the architecture and training procedure are deferred to Chapter~6.
