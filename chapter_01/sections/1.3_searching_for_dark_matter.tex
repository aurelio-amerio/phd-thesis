\section{Searching for Dark Matter}
\label{sec:1.3}

The same coupling that governs dark matter annihilation in the early universe also mediates scattering off nuclei in underground detectors and pair-production at particle colliders.
We therefore explore three complementary strategies for detecting dark matter: direct detection, collider searches, and indirect detection.

Direct and indirect detection both observe dark matter in its natural habitat: the galactic halo, where particles drift at non-relativistic velocities of $v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$.
Colliders, by contrast, collide particles at energies potentially exceeding the dark matter mass, accessing a relativistic regime.
The same dark matter--Standard Model coupling can yield very different cross-sections depending on the kinematic regime: velocity-dependent or momentum-dependent suppressions that are negligible at collider energies can reduce the non-relativistic rate probed by direct and indirect detection by orders of magnitude.
An interaction that produces a strong signal in one channel can therefore be entirely invisible in another.
A signal in one experiment combined with a non-detection in another would narrow the type of interaction at play, and together they carry complementary information that no single channel could yield.

This section reviews direct detection and collider searches in turn, with emphasis on the physical principles rather than experimental details.
We defer a more detailed discussion of indirect detection --- which is particularly relevant for this thesis --- to Section~\ref{sec:1.4}.

\subsection{Direct Detection}
\label{sec:1.3.1}

Direct detection experiments search for the faint recoil produced when a dark matter particle from the galactic halo scatters off an atomic nucleus inside an underground detector.
We restrict the discussion to WIMP--nucleus scattering, being the channel most relevant to this thesis.
A WIMP with mass $M_\chi$ and velocity $v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$ strikes a target nucleus of mass $m_A$ essentially at rest, transferring a recoil energy $E_R \sim \mu_A^2 v^2 / m_A$, where $\mu_A = M_\chi m_A / (M_\chi + m_A)$ is the reduced mass of the system~\cite{Dodelson:2020bqr}.
For a WIMP and a xenon nucleus of comparable mass ($M_\chi \sim m_A \sim 100$~GeV), this yields $E_R \sim 20$~keV --- small enough that detecting it requires highly sensitive instruments, but large enough to be within reach of modern technology.
In addition to the recoil energy spectrum, the Earth's orbital motion around the Sun modulates the WIMP flux by ${\sim}\,5\%$ annually (peaking around June~2), providing a distinctive time-dependent signature~\cite{Drukier:1986tm}.
The DAMA/LIBRA experiment has observed a persistent annual modulation signal in NaI(Tl) crystals over more than two decades~\cite{Bernabei:2018yyw}, but its interpretation as WIMPs is in strong tension with null results from other experiments using different target materials, and the claimed signal remains controversial~\cite{ANAIS-112:2025fne}.

The physics of the scattering divides into two categories determined by the non-relativistic limit of the interaction~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}.
In spin-independent (SI) scattering, the dark matter couples to the nucleon number density without reference to nuclear spin.
In the limit of small momentum transfer --- where the de~Broglie wavelength of the dark matter particle exceeds the nuclear radius --- the amplitudes from individual nucleons add coherently, producing a total cross-section that scales as $\sigma_\text{SI} \propto A^2$, where $A$ is the atomic mass number~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}.
This quadratic enhancement is the primary reason why the most sensitive experiments use heavy target elements, such as xenon ($A \approx 131$).
Spin-dependent (SD) scattering, by contrast, couples to the nuclear spin $J$ and receives no coherent enhancement, leading to bounds that are typically three to five orders of magnitude weaker than the SI case~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The current generation of direct detection experiments is dominated by dual-phase liquid xenon time projection chambers (TPCs), which detect both the scintillation light (S1~signal) and the ionization charge (S2~signal) from a nuclear recoil event~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The ratio S2/S1 provides powerful discrimination between nuclear recoils (the expected dark matter signal) and electron recoils (the dominant background).
Three experiments lead the field: LZ (LUX-ZEPLIN)~\cite{LZ:2022lsv} in the United States, XENONnT~\cite{XENON:2023cxc} at the Gran Sasso laboratory in Italy, and PandaX-4T~\cite{PandaX-4T:2021bab} in China.
Together, they have pushed the upper limit on the SI WIMP--nucleon cross-section down to $\sigma_\text{SI} \lesssim 10^{-47}$~cm$^2$ at $M_\chi \sim 30$~GeV~\cite{LZ:2022lsv,XENON:2023cxc,PandaX-4T:2021bab}.
At higher masses ($M_\chi \sim 100$~GeV), the limits relax to ${\sim}\,10^{-46}$~cm$^2$ as the local number density of dark matter particles decreases~\cite{LZ:2024zvo}.
Beyond these noble liquid TPCs, a complementary program of cryogenic bolometers (SuperCDMS~\cite{SuperCDMS:2020aus}, CRESST-III~\cite{CRESST:2019jnq}) and semiconductor-based detectors (SENSEI~\cite{SENSEI:2020dpa}, DAMIC~\cite{DAMIC-M:2023gxo}) extends sensitivity to sub-GeV dark matter~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Standard Model neutrinos --- solar, atmospheric, and from the diffuse supernova background --- scatter coherently off nuclei and produce recoils kinematically indistinguishable from a WIMP signal; this irreducible background is known as the neutrino fog (or neutrino floor).
The question of whether it would ever show up in data was settled in 2021, when XENON1T recorded the first observation of solar $^8$B coherent elastic neutrino-nucleus scattering (CE$\nu$NS) in a dark matter detector~\cite{XENON:2020gfr}.
PandaX-4T and XENONnT have since reported independent indications at roughly $2.6\sigma$ and $2.7\sigma$, consistent with the expected solar rate~\cite{PandaX:2024muv,XENON:2024hup}; XENONnT also ran the first dedicated dark matter search inside the fog~\cite{XENON:2024hup}, and LZ set the world-best spin-independent WIMP bound while reporting its own $^8$B indications~\cite{LZ:2024zvo}.
What this means practically is that the fog is already a feature of current data: sensitivity gains slow from here, scaling as the inverse square root of accumulated exposure rather than inversely~\cite{OHare:2021utq}, and the next generation of detectors --- notably the DARWIN/XLZD program~\cite{DARWIN:2016hyl} --- will reach multi-ton xenon exposures designed to operate within the fog rather than to avoid it.
Pushing substantially beyond it will likely require directional detectors capable of resolving the apparent ``dark matter wind'' from the Cygnus constellation against the isotropic neutrino background.

Direct detection excels in the mass range where the WIMP mass is comparable to the target nucleus mass --- roughly tens of GeV for xenon targets~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
At lower masses, the recoil energy drops below experimental thresholds; at higher masses, the local number density decreases as $n \propto 1/M_\chi$, reducing the event rate.
These mass-dependent limitations, combined with our entry into the neutrino fog, motivate a complementary search strategy.
Direct detection constrains the WIMP--nucleon scattering cross-section but is insensitive to the annihilation cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ that determines the thermal relic abundance --- the observable probed by indirect detection.

For comprehensive recent treatments of direct detection covering experimental techniques, current bounds, and the path beyond the neutrino fog, we refer the reader to the reviews by Schumann~\cite{Schumann:2019eaa} and Cirelli et al.~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}, and to Hooper~\cite{Hooper:2024}.

\subsection{Collider Searches}
\label{sec:1.3.2}

If dark matter couples to quarks or gluons, it can be pair-produced in high-energy collisions at the Large Hadron Collider.
The challenge is detection: dark matter particles carry no electric or color charge and escape the detector without leaving a trace, just as neutrinos do~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}.
For this reason, they have to be searched for by looking at the missing transverse energy ($E_T^\text{miss}$)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Pure pair-production of dark matter ($pp \to \chi\chi$) produces no visible particles and goes entirely unnoticed.
A detectable signal requires that an additional Standard Model particle --- a jet, a photon, or a $W/Z$ boson --- is radiated from the initial state, recoiling against the invisible dark matter pair.
These are the mono-X searches: mono-jet, mono-photon, mono-$Z$, and mono-Higgs, each paying a cross-section penalty of roughly $g^2/(4\pi)^2 \sim 10^{-3}$ for the additional emission~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
A complementary channel probes \textbf{invisible Higgs decays}: if the dark matter is lighter than half the Higgs boson mass ($M_\chi < M_h/2$), the Higgs can decay directly into a dark matter pair.
Current LHC measurements constrain the invisible branching ratio to $\text{BR}_\text{inv} \lesssim 0.11$ at 95\%~C.L., essentially excluding Higgs-portal thermal relic dark matter below $M_h/2 \approx 63$~GeV~\cite{ATLAS:2023tkt,Cirelli:2024ssz}.
These results are interpreted within \textbf{simplified models} that include an explicit mediator and its couplings to quarks and dark matter, enabling direct comparison with direct and indirect detection bounds~\cite{Boveia:2018yeb}.
Current data exclude $s$-channel mediators in roughly the $0.1$--$4$~TeV range and push the lower bound on colored mediators (squarks, gluinos) to $1$--$2$~TeV~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}.

Despite their power, collider experiments face a fundamental limitation: even if a new invisible particle is produced and characterized, the LHC cannot establish that it is cosmologically stable or that it constitutes the dark matter observed in gravitational systems.

For a comprehensive treatment of dark matter searches at colliders, we refer the reader to the review by Boveia and Doglioni~\cite{Boveia:2018yeb}, and to the broader reviews by Cirelli et al.~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz} and Hooper~\cite{Hooper:2024}.

Among these three strategies, direct detection and collider searches constrain the scattering cross-section, but neither directly measures the annihilation cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle$ that determines the thermal relic abundance.
Because indirect detection is the approach pursued throughout this thesis, we devote the next section entirely to it.
