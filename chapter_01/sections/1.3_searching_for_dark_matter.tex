\section{Searching for Dark Matter}
\label{sec:1.3}

% With the theoretical target established --- a particle in the GeV--TeV mass range whose annihilation cross-section is anchored to the thermal relic value --- the question becomes how to detect it experimentally.
% The answer is not a single experiment but a coordinated campaign that tackles the problem from three distinct directions.
The same coupling that governs dark matter annihilation in the early universe also mediates scattering off nuclei in underground detectors and pair-production at particle colliders.
We therefore explore three complementary strategies for detecting dark matter: direct detection, collider searches, and indirect detection --- see Figure~\ref{fig:detection_triangle}.

Direct and indirect detection both probe dark matter in its natural habitat: the galactic halo, where particles drift at non-relativistic velocities of $v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$.
Colliders, by contrast, collide particles at energies potentially exceeding the dark matter mass, accessing a relativistic regime.
The same dark matter--Standard Model coupling can yield very different cross-sections depending on the kinematic regime: velocity-dependent or momentum-dependent suppressions that are negligible at collider energies can reduce the non-relativistic rate probed by direct and indirect detection by orders of magnitude.
An interaction that produces a strong signal in one channel can therefore be entirely invisible in another.
A signal in one experiment combined with a non-detection in another would narrow the type of interaction at play, and together they carry complementary information that no single channel could yield.

This section reviews direct detection and collider searches in turn, with emphasis on the physical principles rather than experimental details, and closes with the complementary constraints that cosmology places on the annihilation cross-section.
We defer a more detailed discussion of indirect detection --- which is particularly relevant for this thesis --- to Section~\ref{sec:1.4}.

\subsection{Direct Detection}
\label{sec:1.3.1}

Direct detection experiments search for the faint nuclear recoil produced when a dark matter particle from the galactic halo scatters off an atomic nucleus inside a deep-underground detector.
A WIMP with mass $M_\chi$ and velocity $v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$ striking a xenon nucleus of comparable mass transfers a recoil energy of order $20$~keV~\cite{Dodelson:2020bqr} --- small enough that detecting it demands extremely low backgrounds, but large enough to be within reach of modern detectors.
For spin-independent interactions at small momentum transfer, the scattering amplitudes of the individual nucleons add coherently, and the cross-section scales as the square of the atomic mass number, $\sigma_\text{SI} \propto A^2$~\cite{Hooper:2024}; this coherent enhancement is the main reason the most sensitive experiments employ heavy targets such as xenon ($A \approx 131$).

The field is currently led by dual-phase liquid xenon time projection chambers --- LZ~\cite{LZ:2022lsv}, XENONnT~\cite{XENON:2023cxc}, and PandaX-4T~\cite{PandaX-4T:2021bab} --- which have pushed the upper limit on the spin-independent WIMP--nucleon cross-section in a mass-dependent manner, for example down to $\sigma_\text{SI} \lesssim 10^{-47}$~cm$^2$ at $M_\chi \sim 30$~GeV~\cite{LZ:2024zvo}.
These experiments have now reached in sensitivity the \emph{neutrino fog}: Standard Model neutrinos --- solar, atmospheric, and from the diffuse supernova background --- scatter coherently off nuclei and produce recoils kinematically indistinguishable from a WIMP signal, an irreducible background whose onset is already visible in current data through the observation of solar $^8$B coherent neutrino--nucleus scattering~\cite{XENON:2020gfr,PandaX:2024muv,XENON:2024hup,LZ:2024zvo}.
Within the fog, sensitivity improves only as the inverse square root of the accumulated exposure rather than inversely~\cite{OHare:2021utq}, so progress will come slowly even for the multi-ton successors now being planned~\cite{DARWIN:2016hyl}.

Direct detection is most sensitive when the WIMP mass is comparable to that of the target nucleus --- roughly tens of GeV for xenon: at lower masses the recoil energy falls below the experimental threshold, while at higher masses the local number density decreases as $n \propto 1/M_\chi$, suppressing the event rate.
These mass-dependent limitations, together with the entry into the neutrino fog, motivate complementary search strategies.
For comprehensive treatments of direct detection --- experimental techniques, current bounds, and the path through the neutrino fog --- we refer the reader to the reviews by Schumann~\cite{Schumann:2019eaa}, Cirelli et al.~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}, and Hooper~\cite{Hooper:2024}.

\subsection{Collider Searches}
\label{sec:1.3.2}

If dark matter couples to quarks or gluons, it can be pair-produced in high-energy collisions at the Large Hadron Collider.
The challenge is detection: dark matter particles are in general expected to escape the detector without leaving a trace, just as neutrinos do~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
For this reason, they have to be searched for by looking at the missing transverse energy ($E_T^\text{miss}$).

Pure pair-production of dark matter ($pp \to \chi\chi$) produces no visible particles and goes entirely unnoticed.
A detectable signal requires that an additional Standard Model particle --- a jet, a photon, or a $W/Z$ boson --- is radiated from the initial state, recoiling against the invisible dark matter pair.
These are the mono-X searches: mono-jet, mono-photon, mono-$Z$, and mono-Higgs, each paying a cross-section penalty of roughly $g^2/(4\pi)^2 \sim 10^{-3}$ for the additional emission.
A complementary channel probes \textbf{invisible Higgs decays}: if the dark matter is lighter than half the Higgs boson mass ($M_\chi < M_h/2$), the Higgs can decay directly into a dark matter pair.
Current LHC measurements constrain the invisible branching ratio to $\text{BR}_\text{inv} \lesssim 0.11$ at 95\%~C.L., essentially excluding Higgs-portal thermal relic dark matter below $M_h/2 \approx 63$~GeV~\cite{ATLAS:2023tkt}.
These results are interpreted within \textbf{simplified models} that include an explicit mediator and its couplings to quarks and dark matter, enabling direct comparison with direct and indirect detection bounds~\cite{Boveia:2018yeb}.
Current data exclude $s$-channel mediators in roughly the $0.1$--$4$~TeV range and push the lower bound on colored mediators (squarks, gluinos) to $1$--$2$~TeV~\cite{Hooper:2024}.

Despite their power, collider experiments face a fundamental limitation: even if a new invisible particle is produced and characterized, the LHC cannot establish that it is cosmologically stable or that it constitutes the dark matter observed in gravitational systems.

For a comprehensive treatment of dark matter searches at colliders, we refer the reader to the review by Boveia and Doglioni~\cite{Boveia:2018yeb}, and to the broader reviews by Cirelli et al.~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz} and Hooper~\cite{Hooper:2024}.

\subsection{\texorpdfstring{Cosmological Probes: the CMB}{Cosmological Probes: the CMB}}
\label{sec:1.3.3}


An additional methodology to constrain the dark matter annihilation cross-section comes from cosmology~\cite{Lopez-Honorez:2013cua}.
During the cosmic \emph{dark ages} --- the interval between recombination and the formation of the first stars --- dark matter annihilation injects electrons, positrons, and photons that ionize and heat the neutral intergalactic gas; the resulting extra free electrons broaden the last-scattering surface, damping the high-multipole acoustic peaks and enhancing large-scale polarization~\cite{Slatyer:2015jla}.
Because the imprint responds only to the total energy deposited around $z \approx 600$, the \CMB\ constrains a single degenerate combination, $p_\text{ann} = f_\text{eff}\,\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle / M_\chi$, where $f_\text{eff}$ is the fraction of the released energy absorbed by the gas~\cite{Slatyer:2015jla}.
Planck 2018 data bound this combination to $p_\text{ann} < 3.2 \times 10^{-28}\,\text{cm}^3\,\text{s}^{-1}\,\text{GeV}^{-1}$~\cite{Aghanim:2018eyx}, a limit that excludes the thermal relic cross-section for $s$-wave annihilation at masses below roughly $30$~GeV in leptonic channels and $10$--$15$~GeV in hadronic ones~\cite{Lopez-Honorez:2013cua}.
The bound involves only the homogeneous background dark matter density and well-understood atomic and radiative physics, independent of halo profiles and cosmic-ray propagation modelling, and is therefore among the most robust in the field.
That robustness comes at a price: the \CMB\ measures a single amplitude rather than a spectrum, so even a detection would fix neither the dark matter mass nor the annihilation channel without further assumptions, as mass and annihilation channel are degenerate for \CMB probes.
The constraint moreover weakens sharply for $p$-wave annihilation, whose rate is suppressed by the low dark matter velocities of the dark ages~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Taken together, these strategies answer different questions.
Direct detection and collider searches probe the particle nature of dark matter --- its mass and its couplings to the Standard Model --- yet neither can establish its cosmological identity~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
A collider discovery, as noted above, would demonstrate stability only over the time the particle takes to escape the detector, not over the age of the universe.
A recoil signal underground fixes only the product of the local density and the scattering cross-section, so it could equally be produced by a subdominant halo component with an enhanced coupling to nuclei.
The \CMB\ has the opposite limitation: it probes the annihilation of the cosmological dark matter itself, but its single degenerate amplitude cannot resolve the particle behind the signal.
Astrophysical indirect detection bridges the two.
An annihilation signal would originate from the dark matter populating galactic halos and cosmological structures --- the same population whose gravitational effects are reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1}.
Its intensity is set by the cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle$ that determined the relic abundance, while its energy spectrum would discriminate the dark matter mass and annihilation channel.
Since it is the main strategy pursued throughout this thesis, we devote the next section entirely to it.
