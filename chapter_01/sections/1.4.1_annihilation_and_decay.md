## 1.4.1 Annihilation and Decay Physics
\label{sec:ann_decay}

Dark matter particles in present-day galactic halos are cold — their typical velocities are $v \sim 10^{-3}\, c$ — and annihilate or decay essentially at rest \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This kinematic fact has two immediate consequences. First, the total center-of-mass energy available in an annihilation event is simply $\sqrt{s} = 2 m_\chi$, so the most energetic photon that can be produced carries energy $E_\gamma = m_\chi$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. For decay, the available energy is instead $m_\chi$, and momentum conservation among at least two daughter particles limits the maximum photon energy to $E_\gamma = m_\chi / 2$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. These hard kinematic endpoints are one of the most distinctive features of a dark matter signal: no astrophysical process produces a gamma-ray spectrum with such a sharp cutoff tied to a single mass scale.

The second consequence is more profound and concerns the spatial morphology of the expected signal. Annihilation is a two-body process — two dark matter particles must find each other — and its rate per unit volume is therefore proportional to the square of the local dark matter density:
$$
\frac{d\Gamma_\text{ann}}{dV} = \frac{\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle}{2} \frac{\rho^2}{m_\chi^2} \,,
$$
where $\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle$ is the thermally averaged annihilation cross-section and the factor of $1/2$ avoids double-counting for self-conjugate particles \cite{Hooper:2024, Cirelli:2024ssz}. (For Dirac dark matter, where particle and antiparticle are distinct, the factor becomes $1/4$ but the thermal cross-section required for the correct relic abundance is correspondingly larger, leaving the observable rate unchanged \cite{Hooper:2024}.) Decay, by contrast, is a single-particle process with a rate proportional to the first power of the density:
$$
\frac{d\Gamma_\text{dec}}{dV} = \frac{\rho}{m_\chi \tau} \,,
$$
where $\tau$ is the dark matter lifetime \cite{Hooper:2024, Pinetti:2021jjs}. Current gamma-ray observations constrain $\tau \gtrsim 10^{28}$ s \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}, far exceeding the age of the universe — making decay an intrinsically rare process whose detection requires enormous integrated exposures. The $\rho^2$ versus $\rho$ distinction has far-reaching observational consequences: annihilation signals are sharply concentrated in the densest regions of a halo — the inner kiloparsec of the Galactic Center, or the cores of dwarf galaxies — while decay signals are spread diffusely across the entire halo volume, producing a much flatter angular profile \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This morphological difference provides a powerful diagnostic: an excess that tracks the $\rho^2$ profile of the inner Galaxy but vanishes at high latitudes points toward annihilation, while a persistent, broadly distributed signal favours decay.

The stable particles emerging from an annihilation or decay event — photons, neutrinos, electrons, positrons, protons, antiprotons — are determined by the primary channel into which the dark matter annihilates. In a model-independent analysis, one parametrizes the annihilation as $\chi\chi \to f\bar{f}$ for a specific Standard Model final state $f$ (quarks, leptons, or gauge bosons), and the total gamma-ray spectrum is given by a sum over channels weighted by branching ratios \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}:
$$
\frac{dN_\gamma}{dE} = \sum_f \text{BR}_f \, \frac{dN_\gamma^{(f)}}{dE} \,.
$$
In practice, limits are derived for one channel at a time — setting $\text{BR}_f = 1$ for a chosen final state — so each "benchmark channel" maps to a distinctive spectral shape. The spectra $dN_\gamma^{(f)}/dE$ for each channel are computed using Monte Carlo event generators such as Pythia, with the results tabulated in standard libraries such as PPPC 4 DM ID \cite{Cirelli:2010xx, Hooper:2024}. These spectra exhibit qualitatively different shapes depending on $f$. In hadronic channels such as $b\bar{b}$ or $t\bar{t}$, the quarks hadronize into jets containing copious neutral pions, which decay almost instantly via $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$. The resulting gamma-ray spectrum is broad and soft, peaking at $E_\gamma \sim m_\chi / 20$ in $E^2 \, dN/dE$ units \cite{Hooper:2024, Cirelli:2024ssz}. The $b\bar{b}$ channel has become the standard benchmark in Fermi-LAT analyses because it produces a generic spectrum representative of many WIMP models.

Leptonic channels behave differently. The $\tau^+\tau^-$ final state undergoes hadronic decays that produce fewer but more energetic pions, yielding a harder spectrum peaking at $E_\gamma \sim m_\chi / 3$ \cite{Hooper:2024}. Lighter leptons ($e^+e^-$, $\mu^+\mu^-$) do not produce pions at tree level — their gamma-ray spectrum is dominated by final-state radiation and carries far less total flux, concentrated near the kinematic endpoint \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Gauge boson channels ($W^+W^-$, $ZZ$) produce a combination of both behaviours: the hadronic decays of the $W$ and $Z$ generate a broad low-energy bump similar to $b\bar{b}$, while the charged $W^\pm$ bosons also radiate hard photons directly, adding a hard photon tail near $E_\gamma \sim m_\chi$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The underlying pattern is simple: the more hadrons produced in the cascade, the softer the spectrum, because the energy is shared among a large multiplicity of pions. Channels that produce fewer, more energetic particles yield harder spectra with sharper features. In practice, the distinction between the $b\bar{b}$ and $\tau^+\tau^-$ benchmarks brackets the range of spectral shapes relevant for most thermal relic models \cite{Hooper:2024}.
