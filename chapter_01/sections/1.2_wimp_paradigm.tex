\section{The WIMP Paradigm}
\label{sec:1.2}

The evidence assembled in the previous section suggests that dark matter constitutes the dominant form of matter in the Universe and that it cannot be made of Standard Model particles.
This conclusion opens a large parameter space of possible candidates, spanning from ultra-light bosonic fields to macroscopic primordial black holes, with interaction strengths that could range from gravitational to just below current experimental sensitivity.
In this section, we narrow the focus to the framework that has guided the most sustained experimental effort in the field: the paradigm of Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs).

We begin with a survey of the candidate landscape in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.1}, establishing why the Standard Model fails to provide a dark matter candidate and what classes of new particles have been proposed.
Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2} develops the quantitative machinery of thermal freeze-out, deriving the Boltzmann equation for the dark matter abundance and arriving at the central result: the relic density is set by the annihilation cross-section, with the thermal relic value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s.
In Section~\ref{sec:1.2.3}, we use this result to derive the Lee--Weinberg and unitarity bounds that bracket the WIMP mass window to the GeV--TeV range.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:1.2.4} discusses refinements --- velocity-dependent cross-sections, Sommerfeld enhancement, co-annihilation, and resonances --- that can modify the mapping between the cosmological cross-section and the present-day annihilation rate probed by indirect detection.
The thermal relic cross-section, firmly anchored in the physics of freeze-out, will serve as the primary benchmark throughout the indirect detection analyses of this thesis.

The evidence assembled in Section~\ref{sec:1.1} establishes that dark matter exists, that it is roughly five times more abundant than ordinary matter, and that it cannot be made of any Standard Model particle.
The viable parameter space that remains is staggering.
As Figure~\ref{fig:dm_mass_landscape} illustrates, candidate masses span roughly ninety orders of magnitude, from ultra-light bosonic fields near $10^{-21}$~eV, below which the de~Broglie wavelength would exceed the size of observed dwarf galaxies~\cite{Hu:2000ke}, up to macroscopic primordial black holes of tens of solar masses, and interaction strengths range from purely gravitational to just below current direct-detection sensitivity.
Within this space the Standard Model is already excluded: the baryon density measured from Big Bang Nucleosynthesis and the cosmic microwave background, reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1.3}, falls a factor of six short of the total matter density; the Tremaine--Gunn phase-space bound discussed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1.1} forbids any fermionic candidate lighter than about $1$~keV~\cite{Tremaine:1979we}; and hot relics that decouple while relativistic free-stream out of forming potential wells, washing out the small-scale power that observations require~\cite{Bullock:2017xww}.
Dark matter must therefore be a new, non-baryonic, cold or at most warm form of matter, and the open question is which microphysical realization Nature has chosen.

Among the proposed answers, weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) have driven the experimental program for more than two decades.
The reason is the so-called \emph{WIMP miracle}: a stable particle with electroweak-scale mass and weak-scale annihilation cross section is naturally produced by thermal freeze-out at roughly the observed abundance, a coincidence that we develop quantitatively in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2}.
Concrete realizations include the lightest neutralino of supersymmetric extensions of the Standard Model~\cite{Ellis:1983ew} and \emph{minimal dark matter} scenarios in which a single electroweak multiplet, stabilized by an accidental symmetry, plays the entire role of the dark sector~\cite{Cirelli:2005uq}.
The operational appeal for this thesis is more immediate: the same cross section that fixes the relic density also fixes the rate at which present-day WIMPs annihilate into gamma rays inside dense halos, so that the indirect-detection signals studied in Chapters~\ref{ch:4}--\ref{ch:8} probe directly the parameter that thermal cosmology constrains.

Axions are the second candidate that the community currently treats on equal footing with WIMPs, and the steady tightening of direct-detection limits and dwarf-spheroidal indirect-detection bounds has only increased their relative weight.
Axions were originally proposed by Peccei and Quinn~\cite{Peccei:1977hh} as the pseudo-Nambu--Goldstone boson of a spontaneously broken global symmetry introduced to solve the strong CP problem of QCD~\cite{Weinberg:1977ma,Wilczek:1977pj}; the axion acquires a small mass at the QCD phase transition, and an initial misalignment between its field value and the minimum of its potential sources a coherent classical oscillating condensate that today behaves as cold dark matter.
This \emph{misalignment mechanism}, reviewed in~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}, is generic to any light scalar with a late-developing potential and naturally explains why such a feebly coupled particle saturates the observed relic density.
Because axions are bosons they evade the Tremaine--Gunn bound and can sit in occupation numbers $N \gg 1$, behaving as a classical field rather than as a gas of quanta.
The phenomenologically viable range of axion masses is correspondingly broad, spanning roughly $10^{-12}$ to $10^{7}$~eV once astrophysical, cosmological, and laboratory bounds are combined~\cite{OHare:2021utq,Adams:2022pbo}; the open-source compilation maintained by O'Hare and collaborators~\cite{Adams:2022pbo}, complemented by the \texttt{AxionLimits} repository \aure{cite cajohare/AxionLimits Zenodo}, is the standard reference for the current global picture.
Haloscope experiments such as ADMX search for the resonant conversion of the local axion field into microwave photons inside a tunable cavity threaded by a strong magnetic field, and the Snowmass community white paper documents the breadth of the dedicated experimental program~\cite{Adams:2022pbo}.

A separate keV-scale window is occupied by sterile (right-handed) neutrinos, which do not couple to the Standard Model weak interaction but mix slightly with active neutrinos.
Two production mechanisms are commonly considered: the original Dodelson--Widrow scenario of non-resonant oscillations in the early Universe~\cite{Dodelson:1993je}, and the Shi--Fuller mechanism in which a primordial lepton asymmetry drives resonant oscillations and shifts the produced spectrum to colder momenta~\cite{Shi:1998km}.
Resonant production is the more flexible option because it evades the most stringent free-streaming bounds from Lyman-$\alpha$ and Milky Way satellite counts, which otherwise push the allowed mass above $20$--$30$~keV.
Once produced, sterile neutrinos are quasi-stable but decay radiatively as $N \to \nu\gamma$, yielding a monochromatic X-ray line that X-ray telescopes can search for as a smoking-gun signature; the canonical reviews of the model space and its observational status are those of Boyarsky et al.~\cite{Boyarsky:2018tvu} and the keV white paper by Drewes et al.~\cite{Drewes:2016upu}.

Primordial black holes (PBHs) offer a qualitatively different option in which the dark matter is not a new particle at all but a population of macroscopic objects formed from the collapse of large density perturbations before BBN.
Because PBHs are not made of baryons after BBN, they bypass the baryon-budget constraint discussed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1.3}.
Microlensing surveys, dynamical bounds on wide stellar binaries and ultra-faint dwarfs, and CMB spectral-distortion limits together exclude PBHs as the dominant dark-matter component across most of the allowed mass range~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The single surviving window for a monochromatic PBH population to constitute all of the dark matter is the so-called asteroid-mass range, $10^{-16}\,M_\odot \lesssim M \lesssim 3 \times 10^{-12}\,M_\odot$, where Hawking-evaporation bounds from below and microlensing limits from above leave a narrow gap that next-generation experiments aim to close.

A complementary direction for new physics modifies not the identity of the dark particle but its cosmological production history.
In the \emph{freeze-in} scenario, feebly interacting massive particles (FIMPs) couple so weakly to the thermal bath that they never reach equilibrium~\cite{Hall:2009bx}; their abundance is built up gradually through rare decays and $2 \to 2$ scatterings, and crucially it grows with the coupling rather than shrinking, opposite to the freeze-out logic of Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2}~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The viable FIMP mass range is correspondingly broad, and the extreme smallness of the couplings places FIMPs naturally out of reach of direct-detection and collider searches, shifting attention to cosmological and astrophysical probes.

Beyond these production-based classifications, dark matter may also depart from the cold and collisionless idealization on which $\Lambda$CDM is built, and there are reasons to take that possibility seriously.
The persistent small-scale tensions reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1.2} --- the cusp/core problem in dwarf galaxies, the diversity of rotation curves at fixed maximum circular velocity, and the too-big-to-fail puzzle of the Milky Way satellites --- all point to fewer dense, cuspy halos on sub-galactic scales than collisionless simulations predict~\cite{Bullock:2017xww}.
One response is \emph{warm dark matter} (WDM), a kinematic classification of any candidate with a free-streaming length intermediate between hot and cold relics; the resulting suppression of small-scale power softens the small-scale tensions while leaving the large-scale successes of $\Lambda$CDM untouched.
Resonantly produced keV sterile neutrinos are a concrete realization, but WDM is broader, and the astrophysical and cosmological tests that constrain it are reviewed in~\cite{Adhikari:2022sbh}.
A second, orthogonal direction is \emph{self-interacting dark matter} (SIDM), originally proposed by Spergel and Steinhardt~\cite{Spergel:1999mh} as a way to thermalize the inner regions of haloes through elastic scattering and so produce cored, not cuspy, density profiles.
The relevant figure of merit is the cross section per unit mass: values of order $\sigma/M \sim 1~\mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{g}$ at dwarf-galaxy velocities are needed to produce a noticeable astrophysical effect, while bullet-cluster and cluster-scale observations require $\sigma/M \lesssim 1~\mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{g}$ globally and $\lesssim 0.1~\mathrm{cm}^2/\mathrm{g}$ at cluster velocities, motivating velocity-dependent SIDM models with a light mediator~\cite{Kaplinghat:2015aga}.
The status of the program is summarized in the review of Tulin and Yu~\cite{Tulin:2017ara}, and a community of hydrodynamical simulators (the ETHOS framework and the works of Vogelsberger, Rocha, Peter, Fischer, and collaborators) now routinely compares SIDM and WDM predictions to dwarf-galaxy data.

What this catalogue makes clear is how little the existing evidence pins down about the microphysics of the dark sector.
The WIMP paradigm nevertheless retains a privileged status, not because it is theoretically inevitable but because a single number, the velocity-averaged annihilation cross section, simultaneously fixes the cosmological relic abundance, the rate of elastic scattering on nuclei, the production cross section at colliders, and the present-day annihilation rate inside galactic halos.
The remainder of this section develops the thermal freeze-out picture quantitatively and shows why the resulting target value of $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ is the natural setpoint of the experimental program that follows.

\subsection{Thermodynamics of the Early Universe}
\label{sec:1.2.2}

The freeze-out mechanism is the quantitative engine behind the WIMP paradigm.
In this subsection, we derive the Boltzmann equation governing the dark matter number density in the expanding universe, identify the physical conditions under which freeze-out occurs, and arrive at the relic abundance formula that connects the annihilation cross-section to the observed cosmological density.

We consider a stable particle $\chi$ with mass $M_\chi$ that was in thermal and chemical equilibrium with the Standard Model plasma in the early universe through reactions of the form $\chi\chi \leftrightarrow \mathrm{SM}\,\mathrm{SM}$.
At temperatures $T \gg M_\chi$, the dark matter particles are relativistic and as abundant as photons, with number density $n \sim T^3$.
As the universe cools below $T \sim M_\chi$, the particles become non-relativistic and their equilibrium number density becomes exponentially suppressed by the Boltzmann factor~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
	n_\mathrm{eq} = g_\chi \left(\frac{M_\chi T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2} e^{-M_\chi/T} \,,
	\label{eq:n_eq}
\end{equation}
where $g_\chi$ counts the internal degrees of freedom of $\chi$ ($g_\chi = 1$ for a real scalar, $g_\chi = 2$ for a Majorana fermion, etc.)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The time evolution of $n_\chi$ in the expanding universe is governed by the Boltzmann equation~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Gondolo:1990dk},
\begin{equation}
	\frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi = -\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \left(n_\chi^2 - n_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
	\label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
The $3Hn_\chi$ term on the left-hand side accounts for the dilution of the number density due to the Hubble expansion.
On the right-hand side, $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ is the thermally averaged total annihilation cross-section times relative velocity.
The $n_\chi^2$ term describes the depletion of dark matter through annihilations, while the $n_\mathrm{eq}^2$ term accounts for their creation via inverse annihilations from the thermal bath.
When $n_\chi = n_\mathrm{eq}$, the right-hand side vanishes and the system is in chemical equilibrium~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

To factor out the effect of the expanding volume, it is convenient to work with the comoving abundance $Y \equiv n_\chi / s$, where $s = 2\pi^2 g_{*S}(T) T^3 / 45$ is the entropy density and $g_{*S}$ counts the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom contributing to the entropy~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The quantity $g_{*S}$ differs in general from $g_*$, which counts the degrees of freedom contributing to the energy density and enters the Hubble rate~$H$; the two coincide at temperatures well above the MeV scale but diverge after $e^\pm$ annihilation preferentially heats the photon bath relative to neutrinos~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Since freeze-out typically occurs at $T \sim M_\chi/25 \gg 1$~MeV for weak-scale dark matter, we can safely approximate $g_{*S} \simeq g_*$ throughout the freeze-out calculation.
The total entropy in a comoving volume is conserved ($s \propto a^{-3}$), so the ratio $Y$ remains constant whenever the dark matter number per comoving volume is conserved --- both at early times (when $\chi$ is in equilibrium and $Y = Y_\mathrm{eq}$) and at late times (after freeze-out, when annihilations have ceased)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Introducing the dimensionless inverse temperature $x \equiv M_\chi/T$, which increases as the universe cools and equals $\mathcal{O}(1)$ around the time of freeze-out, the Boltzmann equation can be recast as~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
	\frac{dY}{dx} = -\frac{s \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{Hx} \left(Y^2 - Y_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
	\label{eq:boltzmann_Y}
\end{equation}

The physics encoded in this equation leads to a characteristic sequence of events.
At high temperatures ($x \ll 1$), the annihilation rate $\Gamma = n_\chi \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ greatly exceeds the Hubble rate $H \sim T^2/M_\mathrm{Pl}$.
The right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:boltzmann_Y} acts as a restoring force that quickly drives any deviation of $Y$ back to $Y_\mathrm{eq}$: the abundance tracks the equilibrium distribution.
As $x$ increases and the temperature drops below $M_\chi$, the equilibrium density plummets exponentially.
The dark matter particles become so rare that they can no longer find each other to annihilate efficiently.
When $\Gamma$ drops below $H$, the annihilation term shuts off and the comoving abundance $Y$ freezes out at a constant value $Y_\infty$~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The freeze-out condition $\Gamma \simeq H$ determines the freeze-out temperature.
Setting $n_\mathrm{eq}(x_f) \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle = H(x_f)$ and solving iteratively yields~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
	x_f \approx 25 + \ln \left[\frac{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}\right] + \ldots \,,
	\label{eq:xf}
\end{equation}
confirming that for weak-scale cross-sections, freeze-out occurs at $T_\mathrm{fo} \sim M_\chi/25$ --- the dark matter is already well into the non-relativistic regime.

After freeze-out ($x \gg x_f$), the equilibrium term $Y_\mathrm{eq}^2$ is negligible, and the Boltzmann equation reduces to $dY/dx \simeq -f(x) Y^2$, which integrates to~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
	Y_\infty \simeq \frac{x_f}{\lambda} \,, \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{M_\mathrm{Pl} M_\chi \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{1.66 \sqrt{g_*}} \,,
	\label{eq:Y_infty}
\end{equation}
where $g_*$ is the number of effective relativistic degrees of freedom at freeze-out and the numerical prefactor depends on the precise definition of $\lambda$. Converting $Y_\infty$ to the present-day density parameter using $\Omega_\chi h^2 = Y_\infty M_\chi s_0 / \rho_c$ yields~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
	\frac{\Omega_\chi h^2}{0.12} \approx \frac{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_{T \approx M_\chi/25}} \,.
	\label{eq:relic_abundance}
\end{equation}
This is one of the central results in dark matter physics.
The relic abundance depends inversely on the annihilation cross-section and is largely independent of the dark matter mass (which enters only logarithmically through $x_f$)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The numerical constant
\begin{equation}
	\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s} \approx \frac{1}{(23 \, \mathrm{TeV})^2} \approx 0.73 \, \mathrm{pb}
	\label{eq:sigma_v_cosmo}
\end{equation}
is the ``thermal relic cross-section'' --- the value required to reproduce the observed dark matter density~\cite{Steigman:2012nb,Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The physical content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:relic_abundance} can be understood intuitively.
A particle that annihilates more efficiently stays in equilibrium longer, tracking the exponentially falling $Y_\mathrm{eq}$ to lower temperatures before decoupling, and therefore freezes out with a smaller residual abundance.
Conversely, a weakly annihilating species decouples early, when its density is still relatively high, and is left with a larger relic abundance.
In this sense, the ``weakest particle wins''~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The quantitative coincidence underlying the WIMP paradigm is now apparent.
A generic estimate of the annihilation cross-section for a particle with mass $M_\chi$ interacting via a dimensionless coupling $g$ gives $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \sim g^4 / 4\pi M_\chi^2$.
Requiring this to match $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ yields the characteristic mass scale $M_\chi \sim g^2 \times 6.5$~TeV.
For couplings of order the Standard Model weak coupling ($g \sim g_2 \simeq 0.65$), this points to masses in the range of a few hundred GeV to a few TeV --- precisely the electroweak scale~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The fact that a particle with weak-scale mass and coupling naturally yields the correct dark matter abundance, without requiring any fine-tuning, is the ``WIMP miracle''~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
It has served for decades as the primary theoretical motivation for searching for particle dark matter at colliders, in underground detectors, and through astrophysical observations of annihilation products --- the strategy of indirect detection that forms the core of this thesis.

\subsection{Theoretical Bounds on the WIMP Mass}
\label{sec:1.2.3}

The relic abundance formula derived in the previous subsection, combined with basic physical requirements, brackets the viable WIMP mass range from both below and above.
These bounds define the GeV--TeV search window that has guided the experimental program for decades.

\paragraph{The Lee--Weinberg bound.}
A lower limit on the mass of a thermal WIMP follows from requiring that the freeze-out relic density does not exceed the observed dark matter abundance.
For a particle that annihilates via the Standard Model weak interaction in an $s$-wave process, the annihilation cross-section scales as $\sigma_0 \approx G_F^2 M_\chi^2 / 2\pi$, where $G_F = \sqrt{2}\,g_2^2 / 8M_W^2$ is the Fermi constant~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Since the relic abundance is inversely proportional to the cross-section (Eq.~\eqref{eq:relic_abundance}), lighter particles annihilate less efficiently and freeze out with a larger residual density.
The original analysis of Lee and Weinberg~\cite{Lee:1977ua} obtained $M_\chi \gtrsim 2$~GeV for a heavy Dirac neutrino; modern evaluations with updated values of $g_*$ give~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
	M_\chi \gtrsim 3 \, \mathrm{GeV} \qquad \text{(Lee--Weinberg bound)} \,.
	\label{eq:lee_weinberg}
\end{equation}
This bound applies specifically to particles that annihilate through the $W$ and $Z$ bosons of the Standard Model.
Dark matter candidates that interact through alternative mediators --- such as a dark photon or a new scalar --- are not subject to this constraint and can be lighter, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.1}~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

\paragraph{The unitarity bound.}
An upper limit arises from requiring that the annihilation cross-section remains consistent with partial-wave unitarity.
For a generic $s$-wave $2 \to 2$ annihilation process with coupling $g$, the cross-section scales as $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \sim g^4 / 4\pi M_\chi^2$, which decreases with increasing mass.
The maximum value of $g$ is bounded by perturbativity, $g^2 \lesssim 4\pi$, imposing a ceiling on the cross-section for any given mass~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
A particle that is too heavy will have a cross-section that falls below the thermal relic value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$, causing it to freeze out prematurely with an overabundant relic density.
Requiring $\Omega_\chi h^2 \lesssim 0.12$ translates into the constraint~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Griest:1989wd}
\begin{equation}
	M_\chi \lesssim 100 \, \mathrm{TeV} \qquad \text{(unitarity bound)} \,.
	\label{eq:unitarity}
\end{equation}
An independent lower bound at $M_\chi \gtrsim 3$~MeV follows from requiring that the dark matter freezes out before the onset of BBN ($T \sim \mathrm{MeV}$), so that the additional energy density and annihilation products do not spoil the successful predictions for the primordial light element abundances~\cite{Nollett:2014lwa}.

Together, the Lee--Weinberg and unitarity bounds define the mass window
\begin{equation}
	3 \, \mathrm{GeV} \lesssim M_\chi \lesssim 100 \, \mathrm{TeV}
	\label{eq:wimp_window}
\end{equation}
for WIMPs that annihilate through Standard Model weak interactions.
The lower end of this range is accessible to direct detection experiments, while the upper end pushes against the reach of current ground-based gamma-ray telescopes.
For indirect detection through annihilation products, the thermal relic cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s is a natural benchmark: any instrument capable of probing this value directly tests the thermal freeze-out hypothesis.
As we will discuss in Chapter~\ref{ch:4}, the Fermi Large Area Telescope has reached this sensitivity for masses up to several tens of GeV, while the Cherenkov Telescope Array will extend the reach into the TeV domain.

The bounds derived here assume the simplest freeze-out scenario: a single stable particle annihilating through a two-body $s$-wave process with velocity-independent cross-section.
Several physical effects can modify this picture and shift the effective mass range, as we discuss next.

\subsection{Beyond Standard Freeze-out}
\label{sec:1.2.4}

The derivation in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2} assumed a single stable particle annihilating through an $s$-wave, velocity-independent process with perturbative couplings.
Realistic dark matter models often deviate from this minimal picture.
Several physical effects can modify the relationship between the annihilation cross-section during freeze-out and the one relevant for indirect detection today.
Because this thesis is concerned with extracting constraints on $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ from gamma-ray observations, we briefly survey the most important refinements.

\paragraph{Velocity-dependent cross-sections.} In the non-relativistic regime, the annihilation cross-section admits an expansion in powers of the relative velocity~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
	\sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \simeq \sigma_0 + \sigma_1\, v_\mathrm{rel}^2 + \mathcal{O}(v_\mathrm{rel}^4) \,,
	\label{eq:partial_wave}
\end{equation}
where the $\sigma_0$ term corresponds to $s$-wave ($\ell = 0$) annihilation and the $v_\mathrm{rel}^2$ term to $p$-wave ($\ell = 1$).
For $s$-wave processes, the cross-section is essentially constant from freeze-out ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 0.3\,c$) to the present-day galactic halo ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 10^{-3}\,c$).
If the $s$-wave amplitude vanishes --- as can happen due to symmetry arguments such as helicity suppression in Majorana fermion annihilation into light fermions --- the leading contribution is $p$-wave and the present-day rate is suppressed by a factor of $v_\mathrm{rel}^2 \sim 10^{-6}$ relative to the freeze-out value~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
For indirect detection, this suppression renders $p$-wave-dominated channels practically invisible.
In the analyses presented in this thesis, we focus on $s$-wave annihilation channels, where the thermal relic cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ is a meaningful benchmark for indirect detection sensitivity.

\paragraph{Sommerfeld enhancement.}
When dark matter couples to a mediator with mass $M_V \ll M_\chi$, the resulting long-range attractive force distorts the wave function of the incoming pair, enhancing the annihilation probability at low velocities~\cite{Hisano:2004ds,ArkaniHamed:2008qn,Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The enhancement factor $S$ grows roughly as $S \propto 1/v_\mathrm{rel}$ in the regime $v_\mathrm{rel} \ll \pi\alpha_g$ (where $\alpha_g$ is the effective coupling), while it reduces to $S \to 1$ at higher velocities~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
For a massive mediator (Yukawa potential), the enhancement saturates at $v_\mathrm{rel} \sim M_V / M_\chi$ and can exhibit resonant peaks at specific mass ratios.
The key consequence for indirect detection is that the Sommerfeld factor can differ substantially between the freeze-out epoch ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 0.2\,c$, where the enhancement is moderate) and the present-day halo ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 10^{-3}\,c$, where $S$ can reach $\mathcal{O}(10^2$--$10^3)$)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
A dark matter candidate that freezes out with the standard thermal cross-section can thus produce indirect detection signals enhanced by orders of magnitude.
Conversely, the thermal relic cross-section is not always the correct target for indirect searches in Sommerfeld-enhanced models.
We return to this effect in Section~\ref{sec:1.4} and Chapter~\ref{ch:4}, where it impacts the interpretation of the Galactic Center excess.

\paragraph{Co-annihilation.}
If the dark sector contains additional particles with masses $M' - M_\chi \lesssim \text{few} \times T_\mathrm{fo}$, these nearly degenerate states remain abundant during freeze-out and contribute to the depletion of the dark matter density~\cite{Ellis:1983ew,Griest:1990kh,Cirelli:2024ssz}.
The relic abundance is then governed by an effective cross-section that sums over all co-annihilating pairs, weighted by their relative equilibrium abundances~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
	\langle \sigma_\mathrm{eff} v \rangle = \sum_{i,j} \langle \sigma_{ij} v_{ij} \rangle \, \frac{n_\mathrm{eq}^i \, n_\mathrm{eq}^j}{n_\mathrm{eq}^2} \,.
	\label{eq:coann}
\end{equation}
If the heavier partners carry stronger interactions (e.g., color charge in supersymmetric scenarios), they can substantially increase $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ and reduce the relic density below what the lightest state alone would predict.
For indirect detection, however, only the lightest (surviving) species annihilates in the galactic halo, typically with a smaller cross-section than $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$.
Co-annihilation can therefore weaken the connection between the observed relic abundance and the indirect detection signal.

\paragraph{Resonant annihilation.}
When dark matter annihilates through an $s$-channel mediator whose mass sits near $m_\mathrm{med} \approx 2 M_\chi$, the cross-section exhibits a Breit--Wigner resonance~\cite{Griest:1990kh,Gondolo:1990dk}.
The thermally averaged cross-section becomes sharply velocity-dependent: if the resonance lies slightly above $2M_\chi$, the annihilation rate can be enhanced at lower velocities (when the kinetic energy brings the center-of-mass energy onto the pole), potentially boosting the present-day signal relative to the freeze-out value~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Conversely, if the resonance is accessed during freeze-out but not at the lower halo velocities, the late-time signal can be suppressed.
This effect introduces a strong model dependence in the relationship between the thermal relic cross-section and the indirect detection rate, a possibility we discuss further when presenting our upper limits.

These refinements do not invalidate the WIMP paradigm but they complicate the mapping between the cosmological cross-section and the astrophysical signal.
In the standard $s$-wave scenario without Sommerfeld enhancement or co-annihilation, the freeze-out value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s is also the value expected in the galactic halo, providing a clean experimental target.
In more complex models, the relationship is modified in ways that are calculable but model-dependent.
The analyses in this thesis adopt the standard thermal relic cross-section as the primary benchmark while noting where Sommerfeld-enhanced or velocity-dependent scenarios would shift the interpretation.

With the theoretical target established --- a particle in the GeV--TeV mass range annihilating with a cross-section near $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ --- we turn in the next section to the experimental strategies for detecting WIMPs.
