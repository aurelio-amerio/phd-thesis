\section{The WIMP Paradigm}
\label{sec:1.2}

The evidence assembled in the previous section demonstrates that dark matter constitutes the dominant form of matter in the Universe and that it cannot be made of Standard Model particles. This conclusion opens an enormous parameter space of possible candidates, spanning from ultra-light bosonic fields to macroscopic primordial black holes, with interaction strengths that could range from gravitational to just below current experimental sensitivity. In this section, we narrow the focus to the theoretical framework that has guided the most intensive experimental program in the field: the paradigm of Weakly Interacting Massive Particles (WIMPs).

We begin with a survey of the candidate landscape in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.1}, establishing why the Standard Model fails to provide a dark matter candidate and what classes of new particles have been proposed. Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2} develops the quantitative machinery of thermal freeze-out, deriving the Boltzmann equation for the dark matter abundance and arriving at the central result: the relic density is set by the annihilation cross-section, with the thermal relic value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s. In Section~\ref{sec:1.2.3}, we use this result to derive the Lee--Weinberg and unitarity bounds that bracket the WIMP mass window to the GeV--TeV range. Finally, Section~\ref{sec:1.2.4} discusses refinements --- velocity-dependent cross-sections, Sommerfeld enhancement, co-annihilation, and resonances --- that can modify the mapping between the cosmological cross-section and the present-day annihilation rate probed by indirect detection. The thermal relic cross-section, firmly anchored in the physics of freeze-out, will serve as the primary benchmark throughout the indirect detection analyses of this thesis.


\subsection{The Landscape of Candidates}
\label{sec:1.2.1}

The evidence reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1} demonstrates that dark matter exists and is roughly five times more abundant than ordinary matter. Big Bang Nucleosynthesis further proves that it is non-baryonic. Whatever dark matter is, it must be a new form of matter --- but what kind? In principle, the viable mass range spans over 90 orders of magnitude, from ultra-light bosons at ${\sim}\,10^{-21}$~eV --- below which the de~Broglie wavelength exceeds the size of observed dwarf galaxies~\cite{Hu:2000ke} --- to macroscopic objects with masses up to ${\sim}\,10^{4}\,M_\odot$ (above which a single ``particle'' would exceed the mass of the smallest known dark-matter-dominated structures)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Navigating this vast parameter space requires both theoretical guidance and observational constraints to identify the most motivated regions.

We begin by ruling out the Standard Model. Baryonic matter is excluded as the dominant component by two independent arguments: (i) BBN and CMB measurements constrain the baryon density to $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.022$~\cite{Aghanim:2018eyx,Cirelli:2024ssz}, far below the total matter density $\Omega_m h^2 \approx 0.14$; and (ii) baryons were tightly coupled to photons before recombination and could not collapse gravitationally to seed the observed large-scale structure~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Standard Model neutrinos, despite being neutral, stable, and weakly interacting, also fail as dark matter candidates. They are too light ($\sum m_\nu \lesssim 0.12$~eV from CMB and BAO data~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}) to produce the observed relic density. More fundamentally, neutrinos decoupled while still relativistic, making them ``hot'' dark matter: their high velocities would have free-streamed out of gravitational potential wells, suppressing structure formation on galactic scales and producing a ``top-down'' hierarchy of structure formation incompatible with observations~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A related constraint comes from the Tremaine--Gunn bound~\cite{Tremaine:1979we}: the Pauli exclusion principle limits the maximum phase-space density of a fermionic species, requiring any fermionic dark matter candidate to have $m \gtrsim 0.1$--$1$~keV to reproduce the dense halos of dwarf spheroidal galaxies~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Standard Model neutrinos, at sub-eV masses, fall well short of this bound.

With the Standard Model excluded, the search turns to new physics. The candidate landscape can be organized by mass scale and production mechanism.

\paragraph{WIMPs.} Weakly Interacting Massive Particles occupy the GeV--TeV mass range and interact with Standard Model particles through weak-scale couplings. They are produced through thermal freeze-out in the early universe, a mechanism we derive in detail in the following subsection. The observation that a particle with weak-scale mass and coupling naturally yields the observed relic abundance --- the so-called ``WIMP miracle'' --- has made WIMPs the most widely studied dark matter paradigm for over three decades~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Concrete realizations include the lightest neutralino in supersymmetric extensions of the Standard Model~\cite{Ellis:1983ew} and the minimal dark matter framework, in which a single electroweak multiplet with the appropriate quantum numbers is automatically stable and yields the correct relic density~\cite{Cirelli:2005uq}. WIMPs are the focus of this thesis, as their annihilation cross-section is directly testable through indirect detection via gamma-rays.

\paragraph{Axions.} The axion is a light pseudoscalar boson arising from the Peccei--Quinn mechanism~\cite{Peccei:1977hh}, proposed to solve the strong CP problem in QCD~\cite{Weinberg:1977ma,Wilczek:1977pj}. It acquires a small mass around the QCD phase transition, typically in the $\mu$eV--meV range, and is produced non-thermally via the misalignment mechanism: the axion field is displaced from its potential minimum in the early universe and subsequently oscillates coherently, behaving as cold dark matter despite its extraordinarily low mass~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The post-inflationary misalignment scenario points toward masses around ${\sim}\,26\,\mu$eV~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because axions are bosons, they are not subject to the Tremaine--Gunn bound and can have occupation numbers $N \gg 1$, effectively behaving as a classical oscillating field~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Dedicated haloscope experiments such as ADMX search for axion-to-photon conversion in strong magnetic fields.

\paragraph{Sterile neutrinos.} Hypothetical right-handed neutrinos that do not participate in Standard Model weak interactions but mix slightly with active neutrinos constitute viable warm dark matter candidates in the keV mass range~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. They can be produced through non-resonant oscillations (the Dodelson--Widrow mechanism~\cite{Dodelson:1993je}) or resonantly via a primordial lepton asymmetry (the Shi--Fuller mechanism~\cite{Shi:1998km}). The latter allows sterile neutrinos to evade some of the most stringent free-streaming bounds, which otherwise require $m \gtrsim 20$--$30$~keV for non-resonantly produced candidates~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Their slow radiative decay $N \to \nu \gamma$ produces a monochromatic X-ray line that can be searched for with X-ray telescopes.

\paragraph{Sub-GeV dark matter and dark photons.} Models featuring a ``dark sector'' with new light mediators --- such as a kinetically mixed dark photon --- allow dark matter in the MeV--GeV range to achieve the correct relic abundance through annihilation into dark-sector states~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These candidates evade the Lee--Weinberg bound (which applies only to Standard Model weak interactions) and have motivated a new generation of direct detection experiments sensitive to sub-GeV recoils, including SENSEI and DAMIC.

\paragraph{Primordial black holes.} At the opposite extreme, dark matter could consist not of fundamental particles but of primordial black holes (PBHs), formed from the gravitational collapse of large density perturbations in the very early universe, before BBN~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because PBHs form before nucleosynthesis, they are not subject to the baryonic budget constraints. Observational bounds from gravitational microlensing, dynamical effects on stellar systems, and CMB spectral distortions constrain PBHs across most of the mass range. At the time of writing, the ``asteroid-mass window'' $10^{-16}\,M_\odot \lesssim M \lesssim 3 \times 10^{-12}\,M_\odot$ remains the only range where PBHs with a monochromatic mass function could constitute all of the dark matter~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

\paragraph{Freeze-in and FIMPs.} The candidates above are mostly produced either thermally (WIMPs) or via field dynamics (axions). An alternative production mechanism is freeze-in, in which Feebly Interacting Massive Particles (FIMPs) have couplings so small that they never reach thermal equilibrium with the primordial plasma~\cite{Hall:2009bx}. Instead, they are gradually produced through the rare decays or scatterings of bath particles. Unlike freeze-out --- where larger couplings produce less dark matter --- freeze-in yields an abundance that increases with the interaction strength~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This mechanism allows dark matter across a vast mass range while naturally evading direct detection and collider constraints due to the extremely weak couplings involved.

This diversity of candidates --- spanning from $10^{-21}$~eV bosonic fields to solar-mass black holes --- reflects the depth of our ignorance about the dark sector. The WIMP paradigm, however, stands out for a practical reason: the same annihilation cross-section that sets the relic abundance through thermal freeze-out is directly accessible to experimental searches via scattering (direct detection), production (colliders), and annihilation products (indirect detection). This testability, combined with the quantitative success of the thermal relic calculation, motivates the detailed treatment of the freeze-out mechanism that follows.


\subsection{Thermodynamics of the Early Universe}
\label{sec:1.2.2}

The freeze-out mechanism is the quantitative engine behind the WIMP paradigm. In this subsection, we derive the Boltzmann equation governing the dark matter number density in the expanding universe, identify the physical conditions under which freeze-out occurs, and arrive at the relic abundance formula that connects the annihilation cross-section to the observed cosmological density.

We consider a stable particle $\chi$ with mass $M_\chi$ that was in thermal and chemical equilibrium with the Standard Model plasma in the early universe through reactions of the form $\chi\chi \leftrightarrow \mathrm{SM}\,\mathrm{SM}$. At temperatures $T \gg M_\chi$, the dark matter particles are relativistic and as abundant as photons, with number density $n \sim T^3$. As the universe cools below $T \sim M_\chi$, the particles become non-relativistic and their equilibrium number density becomes exponentially suppressed by the Boltzmann factor~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
n_\mathrm{eq} = g_\chi \left(\frac{M_\chi T}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2} e^{-M_\chi/T} \,,
\label{eq:n_eq}
\end{equation}
where $g_\chi$ counts the internal degrees of freedom of $\chi$ ($g_\chi = 1$ for a real scalar, $g_\chi = 2$ for a Majorana fermion, etc.)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The time evolution of $n_\chi$ in the expanding universe is governed by the Boltzmann equation~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Gondolo:1990dk},
\begin{equation}
\frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi = -\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \left(n_\chi^2 - n_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
\label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
The $3Hn_\chi$ term on the left-hand side accounts for the dilution of the number density due to the Hubble expansion. On the right-hand side, $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ is the thermally averaged total annihilation cross-section times relative velocity. The $n_\chi^2$ term describes the depletion of dark matter through annihilations, while the $n_\mathrm{eq}^2$ term accounts for their creation via inverse annihilations from the thermal bath. When $n_\chi = n_\mathrm{eq}$, the right-hand side vanishes and the system is in chemical equilibrium~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

To factor out the effect of the expanding volume, it is convenient to work with the comoving abundance $Y \equiv n_\chi / s$, where $s = 2\pi^2 g_{*S}(T) T^3 / 45$ is the entropy density and $g_{*S}$ counts the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom contributing to the entropy~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The quantity $g_{*S}$ differs in general from $g_*$, which counts the degrees of freedom contributing to the energy density and enters the Hubble rate~$H$; the two coincide at temperatures well above the MeV scale but diverge after $e^\pm$ annihilation preferentially heats the photon bath relative to neutrinos~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Since freeze-out typically occurs at $T \sim M_\chi/25 \gg 1$~MeV for weak-scale dark matter, we can safely approximate $g_{*S} \simeq g_*$ throughout the freeze-out calculation. The total entropy in a comoving volume is conserved ($s \propto a^{-3}$), so the ratio $Y$ remains constant whenever the dark matter number per comoving volume is conserved --- both at early times (when $\chi$ is in equilibrium and $Y = Y_\mathrm{eq}$) and at late times (after freeze-out, when annihilations have ceased)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Introducing the dimensionless inverse temperature $x \equiv M_\chi/T$, which increases as the universe cools and equals $\mathcal{O}(1)$ around the time of freeze-out, the Boltzmann equation can be recast as~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
\frac{dY}{dx} = -\frac{s \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{Hx} \left(Y^2 - Y_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
\label{eq:boltzmann_Y}
\end{equation}

The physics encoded in this equation leads to a characteristic sequence of events. At high temperatures ($x \ll 1$), the annihilation rate $\Gamma = n_\chi \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ greatly exceeds the Hubble rate $H \sim T^2/M_\mathrm{Pl}$. The right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:boltzmann_Y} acts as a restoring force that quickly drives any deviation of $Y$ back to $Y_\mathrm{eq}$: the abundance tracks the equilibrium distribution. As $x$ increases and the temperature drops below $M_\chi$, the equilibrium density plummets exponentially. The dark matter particles become so rare that they can no longer find each other to annihilate efficiently. When $\Gamma$ drops below $H$, the annihilation term shuts off and the comoving abundance $Y$ freezes out at a constant value $Y_\infty$~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The freeze-out condition $\Gamma \simeq H$ determines the freeze-out temperature. Setting $n_\mathrm{eq}(x_f) \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle = H(x_f)$ and solving iteratively yields~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
x_f \approx 25 + \ln \left[\frac{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}\right] + \ldots \,,
\label{eq:xf}
\end{equation}
confirming that for weak-scale cross-sections, freeze-out occurs at $T_\mathrm{fo} \sim M_\chi/25$ --- the dark matter is already well into the non-relativistic regime.

After freeze-out ($x \gg x_f$), the equilibrium term $Y_\mathrm{eq}^2$ is negligible, and the Boltzmann equation reduces to $dY/dx \simeq -f(x) Y^2$, which integrates to~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
Y_\infty \simeq \frac{x_f}{\lambda} \,, \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{M_\mathrm{Pl} M_\chi \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{1.66 \sqrt{g_*}} \,,
\label{eq:Y_infty}
\end{equation}
where $g_*$ is the number of effective relativistic degrees of freedom at freeze-out and the numerical prefactor depends on the precise definition of $\lambda$. Converting $Y_\infty$ to the present-day density parameter using $\Omega_\chi h^2 = Y_\infty M_\chi s_0 / \rho_c$ yields~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Omega_\chi h^2}{0.12} \approx \frac{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_{T \approx M_\chi/25}} \,.
\label{eq:relic_abundance}
\end{equation}
This is one of the most important results in dark matter physics. The relic abundance depends inversely on the annihilation cross-section and is largely independent of the dark matter mass (which enters only logarithmically through $x_f$)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The numerical constant
\begin{equation}
\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s} \approx \frac{1}{(23 \, \mathrm{TeV})^2} \approx 0.73 \, \mathrm{pb}
\label{eq:sigma_v_cosmo}
\end{equation}
is the ``thermal relic cross-section'' --- the value required to reproduce the observed dark matter density~\cite{Steigman:2012nb,Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The physical content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:relic_abundance} can be understood intuitively. A particle that annihilates more efficiently stays in equilibrium longer, tracking the exponentially falling $Y_\mathrm{eq}$ to lower temperatures before decoupling, and therefore freezes out with a smaller residual abundance. Conversely, a weakly annihilating species decouples early, when its density is still relatively high, and is left with a larger relic abundance. In this sense, the ``weakest particle wins''~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The quantitative coincidence underlying the WIMP paradigm is now apparent. A generic estimate of the annihilation cross-section for a particle with mass $M_\chi$ interacting via a dimensionless coupling $g$ gives $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \sim g^4 / 4\pi M_\chi^2$. Requiring this to match $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ yields the characteristic mass scale $M_\chi \sim g^2 \times 6.5$~TeV. For couplings of order the Standard Model weak coupling ($g \sim g_2 \simeq 0.65$), this points to masses in the range of a few hundred GeV to a few TeV --- precisely the electroweak scale~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The fact that a particle with weak-scale mass and coupling naturally yields the correct dark matter abundance, without requiring any fine-tuning, is the ``WIMP miracle''~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. It has served for decades as the primary theoretical motivation for searching for particle dark matter at colliders, in underground detectors, and through astrophysical observations of annihilation products --- the strategy of indirect detection that forms the core of this thesis.


\subsection{Theoretical Bounds on the WIMP Mass}
\label{sec:1.2.3}

The relic abundance formula derived in the previous subsection, combined with basic physical requirements, brackets the viable WIMP mass range from both below and above. These bounds define the GeV--TeV search window that has guided the experimental program for decades.

\paragraph{The Lee--Weinberg bound.} A lower limit on the mass of a thermal WIMP follows from requiring that the freeze-out relic density does not exceed the observed dark matter abundance. For a particle that annihilates via the Standard Model weak interaction in an $s$-wave process, the annihilation cross-section scales as $\sigma_0 \approx G_F^2 M_\chi^2 / 2\pi$, where $G_F = \sqrt{2}\,g_2^2 / 8M_W^2$ is the Fermi constant~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Since the relic abundance is inversely proportional to the cross-section (Eq.~\eqref{eq:relic_abundance}), lighter particles annihilate less efficiently and freeze out with a larger residual density. The original analysis of Lee and Weinberg~\cite{Lee:1977ua} obtained $M_\chi \gtrsim 2$~GeV for a heavy Dirac neutrino; modern evaluations with updated values of $g_*$ give~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
M_\chi \gtrsim 3 \, \mathrm{GeV} \qquad \text{(Lee--Weinberg bound)} \,.
\label{eq:lee_weinberg}
\end{equation}
This bound applies specifically to particles that annihilate through the $W$ and $Z$ bosons of the Standard Model. Dark matter candidates that interact through alternative mediators --- such as a dark photon or a new scalar --- are not subject to this constraint and can be lighter, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.1}~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

\paragraph{The unitarity bound.} An upper limit arises from requiring that the annihilation cross-section remains consistent with partial-wave unitarity. For a generic $s$-wave $2 \to 2$ annihilation process with coupling $g$, the cross-section scales as $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \sim g^4 / 4\pi M_\chi^2$, which decreases with increasing mass. The maximum value of $g$ is bounded by perturbativity, $g^2 \lesssim 4\pi$, imposing a ceiling on the cross-section for any given mass~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A particle that is too heavy will have a cross-section that falls below the thermal relic value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$, causing it to freeze out prematurely with an overabundant relic density. Requiring $\Omega_\chi h^2 \lesssim 0.12$ translates into the constraint~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Griest:1989wd}
\begin{equation}
M_\chi \lesssim 100 \, \mathrm{TeV} \qquad \text{(unitarity bound)} \,.
\label{eq:unitarity}
\end{equation}
An independent lower bound at $M_\chi \gtrsim 3$~MeV follows from requiring that the dark matter freezes out before the onset of BBN ($T \sim \mathrm{MeV}$), so that the additional energy density and annihilation products do not spoil the successful predictions for the primordial light element abundances~\cite{Nollett:2014lwa}.

Together, the Lee--Weinberg and unitarity bounds define the mass window
\begin{equation}
3 \, \mathrm{GeV} \lesssim M_\chi \lesssim 100 \, \mathrm{TeV}
\label{eq:wimp_window}
\end{equation}
for WIMPs that annihilate through Standard Model weak interactions. The lower end of this range is accessible to direct detection experiments, while the upper end pushes against the reach of current ground-based gamma-ray telescopes. For indirect detection through annihilation products, the thermal relic cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s serves as a natural benchmark: any instrument capable of probing this value directly tests the thermal freeze-out hypothesis. As we will discuss in Chapter~\ref{ch:4}, the Fermi Large Area Telescope has reached this sensitivity for masses up to several tens of GeV, while the Cherenkov Telescope Array will extend the reach into the TeV domain.

The bounds derived here assume the simplest freeze-out scenario: a single stable particle annihilating through a two-body $s$-wave process with velocity-independent cross-section. Several physical effects can modify this picture and shift the effective mass range, as we discuss next.


\subsection{Beyond Standard Freeze-out}
\label{sec:1.2.4}

The derivation in Section~\ref{sec:1.2.2} assumed a single stable particle annihilating through an $s$-wave, velocity-independent process with perturbative couplings. Realistic dark matter models often deviate from this minimal picture. Several physical effects can modify the relationship between the annihilation cross-section during freeze-out and the one relevant for indirect detection today. Because this thesis is concerned with extracting constraints on $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ from gamma-ray observations, we briefly survey the most important refinements.

\paragraph{Velocity-dependent cross-sections.} In the non-relativistic regime, the annihilation cross-section admits an expansion in powers of the relative velocity~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
\sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \simeq \sigma_0 + \sigma_1\, v_\mathrm{rel}^2 + \mathcal{O}(v_\mathrm{rel}^4) \,,
\label{eq:partial_wave}
\end{equation}
where the $\sigma_0$ term corresponds to $s$-wave ($\ell = 0$) annihilation and the $v_\mathrm{rel}^2$ term to $p$-wave ($\ell = 1$). For $s$-wave processes, the cross-section is essentially constant from freeze-out ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 0.3\,c$) to the present-day galactic halo ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 10^{-3}\,c$). If the $s$-wave amplitude vanishes --- as can happen due to symmetry arguments such as helicity suppression in Majorana fermion annihilation into light fermions --- the leading contribution is $p$-wave and the present-day rate is suppressed by a factor of $v_\mathrm{rel}^2 \sim 10^{-6}$ relative to the freeze-out value~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. For indirect detection, this suppression renders $p$-wave-dominated channels practically invisible. In the analyses presented in this thesis, we focus on $s$-wave annihilation channels, where the thermal relic cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ serves as a meaningful benchmark for indirect detection sensitivity.

\paragraph{Sommerfeld enhancement.} When dark matter couples to a mediator with mass $M_V \ll M_\chi$, the resulting long-range attractive force distorts the wave function of the incoming pair, enhancing the annihilation probability at low velocities~\cite{Hisano:2004ds,ArkaniHamed:2008qn,Cirelli:2024ssz}. The enhancement factor $S$ grows roughly as $S \propto 1/v_\mathrm{rel}$ in the regime $v_\mathrm{rel} \ll \pi\alpha_g$ (where $\alpha_g$ is the effective coupling), while it reduces to $S \to 1$ at higher velocities~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. For a massive mediator (Yukawa potential), the enhancement saturates at $v_\mathrm{rel} \sim M_V / M_\chi$ and can exhibit resonant peaks at specific mass ratios. The crucial consequence for indirect detection is that the Sommerfeld factor can differ substantially between the freeze-out epoch ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 0.2\,c$, where the enhancement is moderate) and the present-day halo ($v_\mathrm{rel} \sim 10^{-3}\,c$, where $S$ can reach $\mathcal{O}(10^2$--$10^3)$)~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A dark matter candidate that freezes out with the standard thermal cross-section can thus produce indirect detection signals enhanced by orders of magnitude. Conversely, the thermal relic cross-section is not always the correct target for indirect searches in Sommerfeld-enhanced models. We return to this effect in Section~\ref{sec:1.4} and Chapter~\ref{ch:4}, where it impacts the interpretation of the Galactic Center excess.

\paragraph{Co-annihilation.} If the dark sector contains additional particles with masses $M' - M_\chi \lesssim \text{few} \times T_\mathrm{fo}$, these nearly degenerate states remain abundant during freeze-out and contribute to the depletion of the dark matter density~\cite{Ellis:1983ew,Griest:1990kh,Cirelli:2024ssz}. The relic abundance is then governed by an effective cross-section that sums over all co-annihilating pairs, weighted by their relative equilibrium abundances~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
\langle \sigma_\mathrm{eff} v \rangle = \sum_{i,j} \langle \sigma_{ij} v_{ij} \rangle \, \frac{n_\mathrm{eq}^i \, n_\mathrm{eq}^j}{n_\mathrm{eq}^2} \,.
\label{eq:coann}
\end{equation}
If the heavier partners carry stronger interactions (e.g., color charge in supersymmetric scenarios), they can substantially increase $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ and reduce the relic density below what the lightest state alone would predict. For indirect detection, however, only the lightest (surviving) species annihilates in the galactic halo, typically with a smaller cross-section than $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$. Co-annihilation can therefore weaken the connection between the observed relic abundance and the indirect detection signal.

\paragraph{Resonant annihilation.} When dark matter annihilates through an $s$-channel mediator whose mass sits near $m_\mathrm{med} \approx 2 M_\chi$, the cross-section exhibits a Breit--Wigner resonance~\cite{Griest:1990kh,Gondolo:1990dk}. The thermally averaged cross-section becomes sharply velocity-dependent: if the resonance lies slightly above $2M_\chi$, the annihilation rate can be enhanced at lower velocities (when the kinetic energy brings the center-of-mass energy onto the pole), potentially boosting the present-day signal relative to the freeze-out value~\cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Conversely, if the resonance is accessed during freeze-out but not at the lower halo velocities, the late-time signal can be suppressed. This effect introduces a strong model dependence in the relationship between the thermal relic cross-section and the indirect detection rate, a possibility we discuss further when presenting our upper limits.

These refinements do not invalidate the WIMP paradigm but they complicate the mapping between the cosmological cross-section and the astrophysical signal. In the standard $s$-wave scenario without Sommerfeld enhancement or co-annihilation, the freeze-out value $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$~cm$^3$/s is also the value expected in the galactic halo, providing a clean experimental target. In more complex models, the relationship is modified in ways that are calculable but model-dependent. The analyses in this thesis adopt the standard thermal relic cross-section as the primary benchmark while noting where Sommerfeld-enhanced or velocity-dependent scenarios would shift the interpretation.

With the theoretical target established --- a particle in the GeV--TeV mass range annihilating with a cross-section near $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ --- we turn in the next section to the experimental strategies for detecting WIMPs.
