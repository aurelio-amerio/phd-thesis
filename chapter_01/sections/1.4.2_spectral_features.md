## 1.4.2 Spectral Features and Signatures
\label{sec:spectral_features}

The continuum spectra described above are broad, featureless apart from a cutoff at $m_\chi$, and not unique to dark matter — astrophysical gamma-ray sources produce similar power-law-like spectra through conventional particle acceleration \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The discriminating power of gamma-ray searches therefore lies in spectral features that have no plausible astrophysical counterpart. Unlike charged cosmic rays, whose spectral features are smeared by propagation through galactic magnetic fields, sharp structures in the gamma-ray spectrum propagate intact from source to detector and can be searched for directly \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Monochromatic lines.** The cleanest such feature is a gamma-ray line: a monochromatic photon emitted at a fixed energy $E_\gamma = m_\chi$ from the process $\chi\chi \to \gamma\gamma$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because dark matter is electrically neutral, this annihilation cannot proceed at tree level and must be mediated by a loop of charged particles, suppressing the cross-section by a loop factor relative to direct annihilation into Standard Model final states \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. The expected flux is correspondingly small. Yet no known astrophysical mechanism produces a sharp line superimposed on smooth backgrounds, which is why the monochromatic signal has long been regarded as a ``smoking gun'' for dark matter \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A related process, $\chi\chi \to \gamma Z$ or $\gamma h$, produces a secondary line displaced to lower energy, $E_\gamma = m_\chi(1 - M_{Z,h}^2/4m_\chi^2)$, providing an additional handle for identification \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. An interesting exception to the generic loop suppression arises in Minimal Dark Matter models, where the non-perturbative Sommerfeld enhancement boosts the loop-induced cross-section to a level comparable with the continuum contribution for a range of dark matter masses \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Virtual internal bremsstrahlung (VIB).** A second sharp feature arises when a photon is radiated from a virtual charged particle mediating the annihilation \cite{Bringmann:2007nk}. Because this emission brings the virtual propagator closer to its mass shell — particularly when the mediator mass is close to $m_\chi$ — the process preferentially produces hard photons near the kinematic limit \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The resulting VIB spectrum has a characteristic saw-tooth shape: rising steeply toward a sharp cutoff at $E_\gamma \lesssim m_\chi$ \cite{Bringmann:2007nk, Cirelli:2024ssz}. Beyond its distinctive spectral signature, VIB has an important kinematic consequence. As a three-body final state, it lifts the helicity suppression that affects two-body annihilation of Majorana fermions into light fermions: the emission of the additional photon removes the strict angular momentum constraints on the two-body process, allowing the annihilation to proceed via unsuppressed $s$-wave \cite{Bringmann:2007nk, Cirelli:2024ssz}. In models where the two-body rate is helicity-suppressed, the VIB cross-section can actually exceed the tree-level two-body rate, making the three-body radiative process the phenomenologically dominant channel \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. **Electroweak corrections.** For TeV-scale dark matter ($m_\chi \gg M_W$), the radiation of $W$ and $Z$ bosons from the annihilation products becomes significant \cite{Ciafaloni:2010ti, Cirelli:2024ssz}. Although this is formally a higher-order process suppressed by the weak coupling, large logarithmic enhancements of the form $\ln(m_\chi / M_W)$ compensate the suppression at high masses \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The consequences are twofold. First, the sharp peaks of monochromatic lines are smeared, and a new continuum of lower-energy gamma-rays ($E_\gamma \ll m_\chi$) appears from the hadronic and leptonic decays of the radiated gauge bosons \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Second, purely leptonic channels ($\chi\chi \to e^+e^-$, $\mu^+\mu^-$) and even the $\gamma\gamma$ channel acquire an antiproton yield that would be entirely absent at tree level, altering the multi-messenger predictions \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These electroweak corrections were incorporated into the standard spectral libraries only after 2013 \cite{Cirelli:2010xx, Cirelli:2024ssz}, and neglecting them leads to qualitatively wrong predictions for heavy dark matter candidates.

**Prompt versus secondary emission.** The gamma-ray production mechanisms discussed so far — pion decay, final-state radiation, lines, VIB — all occur directly in the annihilation or decay event and are collectively termed *prompt emission* \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. Prompt photons propagate freely through the Galaxy and arrive at the detector with their spectral shape intact, because the Galaxy is effectively transparent to gamma-rays below a few hundred TeV \cite{Hooper:2024}.

A qualitatively different contribution arises when the energetic electrons and positrons produced by dark matter propagate through the interstellar medium. These leptons upscatter ambient low-energy photons to gamma-ray energies via *inverse Compton scattering* (ICS) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. The target photon field — the interstellar radiation field (ISRF) — consists of three components: the cosmic microwave background, optical starlight, and dust-reprocessed infrared light \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. To fix orders of magnitude, a 1 TeV electron upscatters a CMB photon to $\sim 1.5$ GeV, or a UV starlight photon to $\sim 150$ GeV \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The propagating $e^\pm$ also emit *bremsstrahlung* radiation when deflected by the electric fields of interstellar gas nuclei, a process that dominates their energy losses in the 1–10 GeV range near the Galactic plane where gas densities are highest \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These processes produce *secondary* gamma-ray emission that is spatially extended and spectrally softer than the prompt component, and whose morphology depends on the magnetic field structure, gas distribution, and radiation fields of the host galaxy \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}. Secondary emission is sometimes called ``doubly indirect'' because it depends not only on the dark matter distribution but also on the astrophysical environment that reprocesses the electrons \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Despite this additional layer of modelling uncertainty, secondary emission plays a central role in the interpretation of the Galactic Center excess discussed in Chapter~\ref{ch:4}. The very identification of the excess relies on subtracting the diffuse gamma-ray background dominated by cosmic-ray ICS and bremsstrahlung in the dense inner Galaxy \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Hooper:2024}. Indeed, ICS constraints from peaked density profiles have been used to exclude leptophilic dark matter interpretations of the signal \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
