## 1.2.2 Thermodynamics of the Early Universe

The freeze-out mechanism is the quantitative engine behind the WIMP paradigm. In this subsection, we derive the Boltzmann equation governing the dark matter number density in the expanding universe, identify the physical conditions under which freeze-out occurs, and arrive at the relic abundance formula that connects the annihilation cross-section to the observed cosmological density.

We consider a stable particle $\chi$ with mass $M$ that was in thermal and chemical equilibrium with the Standard Model plasma in the early universe through reactions of the form $\chi\chi \leftrightarrow \mathrm{SM}\,\mathrm{SM}$. At temperatures $T \gg M$, the dark matter particles are relativistic and as abundant as photons, with number density $n \sim T^3$. As the universe cools below $T \sim M$, the particles become non-relativistic and their equilibrium number density becomes exponentially suppressed by the Boltzmann factor \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz},
\begin{equation}
n_\mathrm{eq} = g_\chi \left(\frac{MT}{2\pi}\right)^{3/2} e^{-M/T} \,,
\label{eq:n_eq}
\end{equation}
where $g_\chi$ counts the internal degrees of freedom of $\chi$ ($g_\chi = 1$ for a real scalar, $g_\chi = 2$ for a Majorana fermion, etc.) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The time evolution of $n_\chi$ in the expanding universe is governed by the Boltzmann equation \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Gondolo:1990dk},
\begin{equation}
\frac{dn_\chi}{dt} + 3H n_\chi = -\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \left(n_\chi^2 - n_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
\label{eq:boltzmann}
\end{equation}
The $3Hn_\chi$ term on the left-hand side accounts for the dilution of the number density due to the Hubble expansion. On the right-hand side, $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ is the thermally averaged total annihilation cross-section times relative velocity. The $n_\chi^2$ term describes the depletion of dark matter through annihilations, while the $n_\mathrm{eq}^2$ term accounts for their creation via inverse annihilations from the thermal bath. When $n_\chi = n_\mathrm{eq}$, the right-hand side vanishes and the system is in chemical equilibrium \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

To factor out the effect of the expanding volume, it is convenient to work with the comoving abundance $Y \equiv n_\chi / s$, where $s = 2\pi^2 g_{*S}(T) T^3 / 45$ is the entropy density and $g_{*S}$ counts the effective number of relativistic degrees of freedom contributing to the entropy \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Since the total entropy in a comoving volume is conserved ($s \propto a^{-3}$), the ratio $Y$ remains constant whenever the dark matter number per comoving volume is conserved — which is the case both at early times (when $\chi$ is in equilibrium and $Y = Y_\mathrm{eq}$) and at late times (after freeze-out, when annihilations have ceased) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Introducing the dimensionless inverse temperature $x \equiv M/T$, which increases as the universe cools and equals $\mathcal{O}(1)$ around the time of freeze-out, the Boltzmann equation can be recast as \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
\frac{dY}{dx} = -\frac{s \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{Hx} \left(Y^2 - Y_\mathrm{eq}^2\right) \,.
\label{eq:boltzmann_Y}
\end{equation}

The physics encoded in this equation leads to a characteristic sequence of events. At high temperatures ($x \ll 1$), the annihilation rate $\Gamma = n_\chi \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle$ greatly exceeds the Hubble rate $H \sim T^2/M_\mathrm{Pl}$. The right-hand side of Eq.~\eqref{eq:boltzmann_Y} acts as a restoring force that quickly drives any deviation of $Y$ back to $Y_\mathrm{eq}$: the abundance tracks the equilibrium distribution. As $x$ increases and the temperature drops below $M$, the equilibrium density plummets exponentially. The dark matter particles become so rare that they can no longer find each other to annihilate efficiently. When $\Gamma$ drops below $H$, the annihilation term shuts off and the comoving abundance $Y$ freezes out at a constant value $Y_\infty$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The freeze-out condition $\Gamma \simeq H$ determines the freeze-out temperature. Setting $n_\mathrm{eq}(x_f) \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle = H(x_f)$ and solving iteratively yields \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
x_f \approx 25 + \ln \left[\frac{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}\right] + \ldots \,,
\label{eq:xf}
\end{equation}
confirming that for weak-scale cross-sections, freeze-out occurs at $T_\mathrm{fo} \sim M/25$ — the dark matter is already well into the non-relativistic regime.

After freeze-out ($x \gg x_f$), the equilibrium term $Y_\mathrm{eq}^2$ is negligible, and the Boltzmann equation reduces to $dY/dx \simeq -f(x) Y^2$, which integrates to \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
Y_\infty \simeq \frac{x_f}{\lambda} \,, \qquad \lambda \equiv \frac{M_\mathrm{Pl} M \langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle}{1.66 \sqrt{g_*}} \,,
\label{eq:Y_infty}
\end{equation}
where $g_*$ is the number of effective relativistic degrees of freedom at freeze-out and the numerical prefactor depends on the precise definition of $\lambda$. Converting $Y_\infty$ to the present-day density parameter using $\Omega_\chi h^2 = Y_\infty M s_0 / \rho_c$ yields \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Omega_\chi h^2}{0.12} \approx \frac{2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s}}{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_{T \approx M/25}} \,.
\label{eq:relic_abundance}
\end{equation}
This is one of the most important results in dark matter physics. The relic abundance depends inversely on the annihilation cross-section and is largely independent of the dark matter mass (which enters only logarithmically through $x_f$) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The numerical constant
\begin{equation}
\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo} \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26} \, \mathrm{cm}^3/\mathrm{s} \approx \frac{1}{(23 \, \mathrm{TeV})^2} \approx 0.73 \, \mathrm{pb}
\label{eq:sigma_v_cosmo}
\end{equation}
is the "thermal relic cross-section" — the value required to reproduce the observed dark matter density \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The physical content of Eq.~\eqref{eq:relic_abundance} can be understood intuitively. A particle that annihilates more efficiently stays in equilibrium longer, tracking the exponentially falling $Y_\mathrm{eq}$ to lower temperatures before decoupling, and therefore freezes out with a smaller residual abundance. Conversely, a weakly annihilating species decouples early, when its density is still relatively high, and is left with a larger relic abundance. In this sense, the "weakest particle wins" \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The quantitative coincidence underlying the WIMP paradigm is now apparent. A generic estimate of the annihilation cross-section for a particle with mass $M$ interacting via a dimensionless coupling $g$ gives $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle \sim g^4 / 4\pi M^2$. Requiring this to match $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel} \rangle_\mathrm{cosmo}$ yields the characteristic mass scale $M \sim g^2 \times 6.5$ TeV. For couplings of order the Standard Model weak coupling ($g \sim g_2 \simeq 0.65$), this points to masses in the range of a few hundred GeV to a few TeV — precisely the electroweak scale \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The fact that a particle with weak-scale mass and coupling naturally yields the correct dark matter abundance, without requiring any fine-tuning, is the "WIMP miracle" \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. It has served for decades as the primary theoretical motivation for searching for particle dark matter at colliders, in underground detectors, and through astrophysical observations of annihilation products — the strategy of indirect detection that forms the core of this thesis.
