## 1.4.6 Multi-Messenger Context
\label{sec:multi_messenger}

Dark matter annihilation and decay produce all stable Standard Model particles, not only gamma-rays. Each additional messenger opens a complementary window onto the dark sector, but none offers the same combination of directional, spectral, and morphological information that makes gamma-rays the preferred channel for the analyses in this thesis \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Neutrinos.** Like photons, neutrinos are electrically neutral and propagate along straight lines, preserving the directional information that charged cosmic rays lose \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Hooper:2024}. They can also escape environments that are completely opaque to photons. The most important example is the solar interior: dark matter particles from the Galactic halo occasionally scatter off nuclei in the Sun, lose enough kinetic energy to become gravitationally bound, and gradually sink to the core \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. There they accumulate and annihilate, producing neutrinos that — after oscillations and interactions within the dense solar medium — are the only annihilation products that reach the surface \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A detection of high-energy neutrinos from the center of the Sun would therefore probe the dark matter scattering cross-section on nuclei, the same quantity measured by underground direct detection experiments \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Searches by IceCube, ANTARES, Super-Kamiokande, and the future KM3NeT and Hyper-Kamiokande have so far found no signal, but the resulting bounds on the spin-dependent scattering cross-section are approximately one order of magnitude stronger than those from direct detection experiments \cite{IceCube:2016dgk, Cirelli:2024ssz}. For spin-independent scattering, the sensitivity is reversed: the neutrino constraints are about two orders of magnitude weaker than current direct detection limits \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. In galactic searches — from the Galactic Center, halo, and dwarf galaxies — neutrino constraints are generally weaker than the corresponding gamma-ray bounds, but become competitive for dark matter masses above several TeV, because the neutrino interaction cross-section grows with energy and partly compensates for the reduced flux \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Charged cosmic rays.** Positrons, antiprotons, and heavier anti-nuclei are produced abundantly in dark matter annihilation, and the astrophysical backgrounds for antimatter are far smaller than for the corresponding matter species — which is why dark matter searches concentrate on antiparticles rather than on the more abundant protons and electrons \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The fundamental limitation of all charged messengers is that galactic magnetic fields randomize their arrival directions, erasing the spatial information that makes gamma-ray searches so powerful and leaving only the energy spectrum as a diagnostic \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Pinetti:2021jjs}.

Positrons lose energy rapidly through synchrotron radiation and inverse Compton scattering as they propagate, so that the detected flux is dominated by local sources: roughly 90\% of 1 TeV positrons reaching Earth originate from within 1 kpc \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The steep rise in the cosmic-ray positron fraction above 10 GeV, first measured by PAMELA and confirmed with high precision by AMS-02, initially attracted considerable attention as a potential dark matter signal \cite{PAMELA:2008gwm, Cirelli:2024ssz}. Fitting the excess with annihilating dark matter, however, requires a multi-TeV particle with a cross-section of order $\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle \sim 10^{-23}$ cm$^3$/s — three orders of magnitude above the thermal relic value — and purely leptophilic couplings to avoid overproducing antiprotons \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These requirements are in severe tension with gamma-ray constraints from dwarf galaxies and with CMB bounds on energy injection during recombination \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Today, the positron excess is widely attributed to nearby pulsars: the intense magnetic fields of rapidly spinning neutron stars strip electrons from the surface, producing $e^\pm$ pairs whose energy budget and spectral shape match the observed data without the tensions that plague the dark matter interpretation \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Hooper:2024}.

Antiprotons undergo negligible energy losses and thus preserve their injection spectrum, probing a much larger galactic volume than positrons \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. AMS-02 data have revealed a potential excess of antiprotons at kinetic energies of 10–20 GeV, which can be fit by an $\sim$80 GeV dark matter particle annihilating into $b\bar{b}$ with a roughly thermal cross-section \cite{Cuoco:2016eej, Cirelli:2024ssz} — properties tantalizingly close to those invoked to explain the Galactic Center gamma-ray excess (Chapter~\ref{ch:4}). However, predictions of the low-energy antiproton spectrum are sensitive to the propagation model, the treatment of solar modulation, and the antiproton production cross-sections. When these systematic uncertainties are properly accounted for, several comprehensive analyses find the data to be consistent with a purely secondary astrophysical origin, and the global significance of the excess drops below $\sim 2\sigma$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Anti-nuclei.** A qualitatively different and potentially decisive measurement would be the detection of anti-deuterons or anti-helium at low kinetic energies. Heavier anti-nuclei are produced when antinucleons from a dark matter annihilation event coalesce — that is, when an antiproton and an antineutron (or more antinucleons, for anti-helium) are emitted with momenta sufficiently close to merge into a bound state \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The yield is heavily suppressed, roughly a factor of $\mathcal{O}(10^{-5})$ per additional antinucleon, but the signal benefits from an almost vanishing astrophysical background \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Standard secondary anti-deuterons, produced by spallation of high-energy cosmic rays on interstellar gas, peak at a few GeV per nucleon, while dark matter annihilation — occurring essentially at rest — produces anti-nuclei that peak below 1 GeV per nucleon, a regime where the background is practically zero \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Hooper:2024}. Current bounds from the BESS balloon experiment are too weak to constrain dark matter \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}, but the projected sensitivities of AMS-02 and the upcoming GAPS experiment — a balloon-borne detector that identifies anti-deuterons by capturing them into exotic atoms whose annihilation emits characteristic X-rays and pions — may reach the thermal relic cross-section for 100 GeV dark matter under favorable conditions \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. AMS-02 has also tentatively reported $\sim$9 anti-helium candidate events \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. If confirmed, this observation would be difficult to explain with either standard astrophysical production or current dark matter models, both of which predict fluxes many orders of magnitude below the detector sensitivity \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

This thesis focuses exclusively on **gamma-rays** — the messenger that provides both spatial and spectral information while relying on the fewest astrophysical assumptions. The multi-messenger context is noted where relevant, but the analyses of Parts II–V are rooted entirely in the gamma-ray formalism developed in this section.
