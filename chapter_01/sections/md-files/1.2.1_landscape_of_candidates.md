## 1.2.1 The Landscape of Candidates

The evidence reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:1.1} demonstrates that dark matter exists and is roughly five times more abundant than ordinary matter. Big Bang Nucleosynthesis further proves that it is non-baryonic. Whatever dark matter is, it must be a new form of matter — but what kind? In principle, the viable mass range spans over 90 orders of magnitude, from ultra-light bosons at $\sim 10^{-21}$ eV (below which the de Broglie wavelength exceeds the size of observed dwarf galaxies) to macroscopic objects with masses up to $\sim 10^{4}\,M_\odot$ (above which a single "particle" would exceed the mass of the smallest known dark-matter-dominated structures) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Navigating this vast parameter space requires both theoretical guidance and observational constraints to identify the most motivated regions.

We begin by ruling out the Standard Model. Baryonic matter is excluded as the dominant component by two independent arguments: (i) BBN and CMB measurements constrain the baryon density to $\Omega_b h^2 \approx 0.022$ \cite{Aghanim:2018eyx,Cirelli:2024ssz}, far below the total matter density $\Omega_m h^2 \approx 0.14$; and (ii) baryons were tightly coupled to photons before recombination and could not collapse gravitationally to seed the observed large-scale structure \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Standard Model neutrinos, despite being neutral, stable, and weakly interacting, also fail as dark matter candidates. They are too light ($\sum m_\nu \lesssim 0.12$ eV from CMB and BAO data \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}) to produce the observed relic density. More fundamentally, neutrinos decoupled while still relativistic, making them "hot" dark matter: their high velocities would have free-streamed out of gravitational potential wells, suppressing structure formation on galactic scales and producing a "top-down" hierarchy of structure formation incompatible with observations \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A related constraint comes from the Tremaine--Gunn bound \cite{Tremaine:1979we}: the Pauli exclusion principle limits the maximum phase-space density of a fermionic species, requiring any fermionic dark matter candidate to have $m \gtrsim 0.1$--$1$ keV to reproduce the dense halos of dwarf spheroidal galaxies \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Standard Model neutrinos, at sub-eV masses, fall well short of this bound.

With the Standard Model excluded, the search turns to new physics. The candidate landscape can be organized by mass scale and production mechanism.

**WIMPs.** Weakly Interacting Massive Particles occupy the GeV--TeV mass range and interact with Standard Model particles through weak-scale couplings. They are produced through thermal freeze-out in the early universe, a mechanism we derive in detail in the following subsection. The remarkable observation that a particle with weak-scale mass and coupling naturally yields the observed relic abundance — the so-called "WIMP miracle" — has made WIMPs the most widely studied dark matter paradigm for over three decades \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Concrete realizations include the lightest neutralino in supersymmetric extensions of the Standard Model \cite{Ellis:1983ew} and the minimal dark matter framework, in which a single electroweak multiplet with the appropriate quantum numbers is automatically stable and yields the correct relic density \cite{Cirelli:2005uq}. WIMPs are the focus of this thesis, as their annihilation cross-section is directly testable through indirect detection via gamma-rays.

**Axions.** The axion is a light pseudoscalar boson originally postulated to solve the strong CP problem in QCD \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. It acquires a small mass around the QCD phase transition, typically in the $\mu$eV--meV range, and is produced non-thermally via the misalignment mechanism: the axion field is displaced from its potential minimum in the early universe and subsequently oscillates coherently, behaving as cold dark matter despite its extraordinarily low mass \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The post-inflationary misalignment scenario points toward masses around $\sim 26\,\mu$eV \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because axions are bosons, they are not subject to the Tremaine--Gunn bound and can have occupation numbers $N \gg 1$, effectively behaving as a classical oscillating field \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Dedicated haloscope experiments such as ADMX search for axion-to-photon conversion in strong magnetic fields.

**Sterile neutrinos.** Hypothetical right-handed neutrinos that do not participate in Standard Model weak interactions but mix slightly with active neutrinos constitute viable warm dark matter candidates in the keV mass range \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. They can be produced through non-resonant oscillations (the Dodelson--Widrow mechanism \cite{Dodelson:1993je}) or resonantly via a primordial lepton asymmetry (the Shi--Fuller mechanism \cite{Shi:1998km}). The latter allows sterile neutrinos to evade some of the most stringent free-streaming bounds, which otherwise require $m \gtrsim 20$--$30$ keV for non-resonantly produced candidates \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Their slow radiative decay $N \to \nu \gamma$ produces a monochromatic X-ray line that can be searched for with X-ray telescopes.

**Sub-GeV dark matter and dark photons.** Models featuring a "dark sector" with new light mediators — such as a kinetically mixed dark photon — allow dark matter in the MeV--GeV range to achieve the correct relic abundance through annihilation into dark-sector states \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These candidates evade the Lee--Weinberg bound (which applies only to Standard Model weak interactions) and have motivated a new generation of direct detection experiments sensitive to sub-GeV recoils, including SENSEI and DAMIC.

**Primordial black holes.** At the opposite extreme, dark matter could consist not of fundamental particles but of primordial black holes (PBHs), formed from the gravitational collapse of large density perturbations in the very early universe, before BBN \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because PBHs form before nucleosynthesis, they are not subject to the baryonic budget constraints. Observational bounds from gravitational microlensing, dynamical effects on stellar systems, and CMB spectral distortions constrain PBHs across most of the mass range. At the time of writing, the "asteroid-mass window" $10^{-16}\,M_\odot \lesssim M \lesssim 3 \times 10^{-12}\,M_\odot$ remains the only range where PBHs with a monochromatic mass function could constitute all of the dark matter \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

**Freeze-in and FIMPs.** The candidates above are mostly produced either thermally (WIMPs) or via field dynamics (axions). An alternative production mechanism is freeze-in, in which Feebly Interacting Massive Particles (FIMPs) have couplings so small that they never reach thermal equilibrium with the primordial plasma \cite{Hall:2009bx}. Instead, they are gradually produced through the rare decays or scatterings of bath particles. Unlike freeze-out — where larger couplings produce less dark matter — freeze-in yields an abundance that increases with the interaction strength \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This mechanism allows dark matter across a vast mass range while naturally evading direct detection and collider constraints due to the extremely weak couplings involved.

This diversity of candidates — spanning from $10^{-21}$ eV bosonic fields to solar-mass black holes — reflects the depth of our ignorance about the dark sector. The WIMP paradigm, however, stands out for a practical reason: the same annihilation cross-section that sets the relic abundance through thermal freeze-out is directly accessible to experimental searches via scattering (direct detection), production (colliders), and annihilation products (indirect detection). This testability, combined with the quantitative success of the thermal relic calculation, motivates the detailed treatment of the freeze-out mechanism that follows.
