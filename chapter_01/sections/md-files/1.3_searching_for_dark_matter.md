With the theoretical target established — a particle in the GeV–TeV mass range whose annihilation cross-section is anchored to the thermal relic value — the question becomes: how do we actually detect it? The answer, it turns out, is not a single experiment but a coordinated experimental campaign that attacks the problem from three distinct directions. If dark matter interacts with ordinary matter through any non-gravitational force, then the same coupling that governs annihilation in the early universe also mediates scattering in underground laboratories and production at particle colliders. These three approaches — direct detection, collider production, and indirect detection — form what is commonly known as the "detection triangle" \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Each vertex of this triangle probes a different kinematic regime of the same underlying interaction. Direct and indirect detection both observe dark matter in its natural habitat: the galactic halo, where particles drift at non-relativistic velocities of $v \sim 10^{-3}\, c$. Colliders, by contrast, slam particles together at energies far exceeding the dark matter mass, accessing a fully relativistic regime. Because the chiral structure of the dark matter–Standard Model coupling behaves very differently in these two limits, an interaction that produces a strong signal in one channel can be entirely invisible in another \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This is not a weakness of the experimental program — it is its greatest strength. A signal in one corner combined with a null result in another would directly reveal the spin and coupling structure of the dark sector \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A signal in all three would constitute an unambiguous identification.

This section reviews each strategy in turn, with emphasis on the physical principles rather than experimental details. We close with the argument for why indirect detection via gamma-rays — the approach pursued throughout this thesis — provides the most direct test of the WIMP hypothesis.


## 1.3.1 Direct Detection

Direct detection experiments search for the faint recoil produced when a dark matter particle from the galactic halo scatters off an atomic nucleus inside a carefully shielded underground detector \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The kinematics are straightforward: a WIMP with mass $M_\chi$ and velocity $v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$ strikes a target nucleus of mass $m_A$ essentially at rest, transferring a recoil energy $E_R \sim \mu_A^2 v^2 / m_A$, where $\mu_A = M_\chi m_A / (M_\chi + m_A)$ is the reduced mass of the system \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. For a WIMP and a xenon nucleus of comparable mass ($M_\chi \sim m_A \sim 100$ GeV), this yields $E_R \sim 20$ keV — small enough that detecting it requires extraordinarily sensitive instruments, but large enough to be within reach of modern technology.

The physics of the scattering divides cleanly into two categories determined by the non-relativistic limit of the interaction \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}. In **spin-independent (SI)** scattering, the dark matter couples to the nucleon number density without reference to nuclear spin. In the limit of small momentum transfer — where the de Broglie wavelength of the dark matter particle exceeds the nuclear radius — the amplitudes from individual nucleons add coherently, producing a total cross-section that scales as $\sigma_\text{SI} \propto A^2$, where $A$ is the atomic mass number \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}. This quadratic enhancement is the primary reason why the most sensitive experiments use heavy target elements, particularly xenon ($A \approx 131$). **Spin-dependent (SD)** scattering, by contrast, couples to the nuclear spin $J$ and receives no coherent enhancement, leading to bounds that are typically three to five orders of magnitude weaker than the SI case \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The current generation of direct detection experiments is dominated by dual-phase liquid xenon time projection chambers (TPCs), which detect both the scintillation light (S1 signal) and the ionization charge (S2 signal) from a nuclear recoil event \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The ratio S2/S1 provides powerful discrimination between nuclear recoils (the expected dark matter signal) and electron recoils (the dominant background). Three experiments lead the field: **LZ** (LUX-ZEPLIN) in the United States, **XENONnT** at the Gran Sasso laboratory in Italy, and **PandaX-4T** in China. Together, they have pushed the upper limit on the SI WIMP-nucleon cross-section down to $\sigma_\text{SI} \lesssim 10^{-46}$ cm$^2$ for a dark matter mass near $M_\chi \sim 90$ GeV \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

These experiments are now approaching a fundamental barrier. Standard Model neutrinos — from the Sun, the atmosphere, and the diffuse supernova background — also scatter coherently off nuclei, producing recoils that can mimic a dark matter signal almost perfectly \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. This irreducible background, known as the **neutrino fog** (or neutrino floor), has qualitatively different consequences depending on the dark matter mass. Below $\sim 10$ GeV, high-flux solar neutrinos generate recoil energies up to tens of keV that overlap with the expected WIMP signal. Above $\sim 100$ GeV, lower-flux atmospheric neutrinos become the limiting factor \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Once an experiment's sensitivity enters the neutrino fog, the improvement in cross-section bounds slows dramatically — scaling as the inverse square root of the exposure rather than inversely with it. Pushing beyond this barrier may eventually require directional detectors capable of distinguishing the apparent "dark matter wind" coming from the Cygnus constellation from the isotropic neutrino background \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Direct detection excels in the mass range where the WIMP mass is comparable to the target nucleus mass — roughly tens of GeV for xenon targets \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. At lower masses, the recoil energy drops below experimental thresholds; at higher masses, the local number density of dark matter particles decreases as $n \propto 1/M_\chi$, reducing the event rate. These mass-dependent limitations, combined with the approaching neutrino fog, motivate a multi-pronged search strategy.


## 1.3.2 Collider Searches

If dark matter couples to quarks or gluons, it can be pair-produced in the high-energy collisions at the Large Hadron Collider. The challenge is detection: dark matter particles carry no electric or color charge and escape the detector without leaving a trace, just as neutrinos do \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}. The only unavoidable signature is **missing transverse energy** ($E_T^\text{miss}$), inferred from the momentum imbalance of the visible particles in the event \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Pure pair-production of dark matter ($pp \to \chi\chi$) produces no visible particles and goes entirely unnoticed. A detectable signal requires that an additional Standard Model particle — a jet, a photon, or a $W/Z$ boson — is radiated from the initial state, recoiling against the invisible dark matter pair \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These are the **mono-X searches**: mono-jet, mono-photon, mono-$Z$, and mono-Higgs, each paying a cross-section penalty of roughly $g^2/(4\pi)^2 \sim 10^{-3}$ for the additional emission \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. A complementary channel probes **invisible Higgs decays**: if the dark matter is lighter than half the Higgs boson mass ($M_\chi < M_h/2$), the Higgs can decay directly into a dark matter pair. Current LHC measurements constrain the invisible branching ratio to $\text{BR}_\text{inv} \lesssim 0.11$ at 95\% C.L., essentially requiring thermal relic dark matter to be heavier than $M_h/2 \approx 63$ GeV in Higgs-portal scenarios \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Additional search channels arise in specific models — for instance, the nearly degenerate charged partners predicted by minimal dark matter models produce **disappearing tracks**, high-$p_T$ charged tracks that suddenly vanish when the charged state decays into the neutral dark matter particle and an undetectably soft pion \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

The theoretical interpretation of collider searches has evolved significantly. The initial approach used an **Effective Field Theory (EFT)** framework, integrating out the heavy mediator to produce contact interactions of the form $\mathcal{O} \sim (1/\Lambda^2)(\bar{q}\Gamma q)(\bar{\chi}\Gamma'\chi)$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. While elegant and model-independent, this description breaks down at LHC energies whenever the collision energy approaches or exceeds the mediator mass — precisely the regime most accessible to the experiment \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The community has therefore shifted to **simplified models**, which explicitly introduce the dark matter particle, a mediator (typically an $s$-channel vector $Z'$ or pseudoscalar), and their couplings \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Simplified models capture the resonant enhancement that occurs when the mediator is produced on-shell and allow consistent comparisons with direct and indirect detection constraints \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Simplified models also reveal an important diagnostic: any mediator that couples to quarks strongly enough to be produced at the LHC can also decay back into quark-antiquark pairs, producing a resonance in the di-jet invariant mass spectrum. In practice, these **di-jet searches are often more constraining than the missing energy searches** themselves \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Current LHC data exclude $s$-channel mediators in the mass range $\sim 0.1$–$4$ TeV unless the coupling to quarks is substantially smaller than the coupling to dark matter ($g_q \ll g_\text{DM}$) \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. For strongly interacting colored mediators — such as squarks or gluinos in supersymmetric models — production cross-sections are governed by QCD, and masses below roughly 1–2 TeV have been firmly excluded \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}.

The complementarity between collider and astrophysical searches is rooted in kinematics. In direct and indirect detection, dark matter is non-relativistic ($v \sim 10^{-3}\,c$), and the chiral structure of the interaction plays a decisive role: vector$\times$vector couplings produce coherently enhanced scattering or unsuppressed $s$-wave annihilation, while other structures can be suppressed by powers of the velocity or the momentum transfer \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. At the LHC, collisions are highly relativistic and the center-of-mass energy dwarfs the dark matter mass, rendering the production cross-section essentially insensitive to the chiral structure \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Colliders can therefore probe **``coy'' dark matter** — candidates whose astrophysical signals are accidentally suppressed by velocity or momentum factors — that would evade both direct and indirect detection entirely \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Conversely, a thermal relic detected through its annihilation products in the galaxy but invisible at the LHC would point to a mediator heavier than the collider reach or to an interaction structure that produces on-shell annihilation but suppressed pair production.

Despite their power, collider experiments face a fundamental limitation: even if a new invisible particle is produced and characterized, the LHC cannot establish that it is cosmologically stable or that it constitutes the dark matter observed in gravitational systems. Confirming the cosmological connection — that the particle froze out in the early universe with the correct relic abundance — requires measuring the annihilation cross-section directly. This is the domain of indirect detection.


## 1.3.3 Why Indirect Detection?

Among the three strategies, indirect detection occupies a unique position: it is the only approach that directly probes the annihilation cross-section $\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle$ — the same quantity that determines the thermal relic abundance \cite{Hooper:2024}. For a standard $s$-wave WIMP, the cross-section at freeze-out ($\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}$ cm$^3$/s) is essentially the same as the one operating in today's galactic halos, providing a firm, parameter-free experimental target \cite{Hooper:2024}. Neither direct detection nor collider production offers this direct connection to cosmology.

Dark matter annihilation (or decay) in astrophysical environments produces all stable Standard Model particles — gamma-rays, neutrinos, electrons, positrons, antiprotons, and heavier anti-nuclei — each carrying information about the mass, coupling, and spatial distribution of the dark sector \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Among these messengers, **gamma-rays** occupy a privileged position. Photons are electrically neutral and travel in straight lines, preserving both the angular distribution and the spectral signature of their source \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Pinetti:2022}, whereas charged cosmic rays — positrons, antiprotons — are deflected by galactic magnetic fields, erasing all directional information and distorting the energy spectrum through diffusion and energy losses \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The universe is also largely transparent to gamma-rays up to TeV-scale energies over galactic distances, so they reach the detector without significant absorption \cite{Hooper:2024}. Neutrinos share the advantage of being neutral and undeflected, but their interaction cross-sections are so small that detecting them requires cubic-kilometer-scale instruments and still yields far fewer events than a gamma-ray telescope of comparable collecting area \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Gamma-rays therefore provide the cleanest window on dark matter annihilation: they carry uncontaminated morphological information (where the dark matter is concentrated) and spectral information (the dark matter mass and annihilation channel).

The promise of gamma-ray indirect detection is already supported by suggestive — if contested — observational evidence. The **Galactic Center** is the brightest expected dark matter target, owing to its proximity ($\sim 8$ kpc) and the high dark matter density predicted by cuspy halo profiles \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Pinetti:2022}. Observations by the Fermi Large Area Telescope have revealed a statistically significant excess of gamma-rays between 1 and 5 GeV, extending spherically to $\sim 10$–$20^\circ$ from the Galactic Center \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Hooper:2024}. The spectrum and morphology are consistent with a $\sim 40$–$70$ GeV dark matter particle annihilating into $b\bar{b}$ at a rate matching the thermal relic cross-section \cite{Hooper:2024}. However, the Galactic Center is also plagued by intense and imperfectly modeled astrophysical backgrounds — diffuse emission from cosmic ray interactions with dense gas, unresolved point sources, and past outburst activity — and the leading alternative explanation invokes an unresolved population of millisecond pulsars in the Galactic bulge \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Pinetti:2022}. The origin of this excess remains vigorously debated and constitutes one of the central motivations for the analyses presented in Chapter~\ref{ch:4}.

To circumvent the overwhelming backgrounds of the Galactic Center, astronomers have turned to **dwarf spheroidal galaxies** (dSphs) — satellite galaxies of the Milky Way that are among the most dark-matter-dominated objects known, with mass-to-light ratios reaching into the thousands \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz,Pinetti:2022}. These systems are essentially devoid of gas, active star formation, and pulsars, producing negligible astrophysical gamma-ray emission. While the expected dark matter flux from any individual dwarf is orders of magnitude smaller than from the Galactic Center, the absence of backgrounds makes them extraordinarily clean targets \cite{Hooper:2024}. By stacking observations of dozens of dSphs, the Fermi-LAT collaboration has derived some of the most robust constraints on the annihilation cross-section to date, excluding the thermal relic value for masses below $\sim 100$ GeV in the $b\bar{b}$ channel \cite{Hooper:2024}. These constraints carry systematic uncertainties related to the dark matter density profiles inferred from stellar kinematics — an issue we revisit in Chapter~\ref{ch:5}.

The complementary strengths of these targets — the Galactic Center's brightness, the dSphs' cleanliness, and the integrated signal from the extragalactic background — define the multi-target observation program pursued in this thesis. Before developing the mathematical formalism of indirect detection in the next section, we summarize the key message of the detection triangle: direct detection, collider searches, and indirect detection probe different kinematic regimes of the same interaction, and the absence of a signal in one channel is information, not failure. The WIMP hypothesis is not falsified until all three vertices have been explored to the thermal relic benchmark. Indirect detection via gamma-rays is the approach that most directly tests this benchmark, and it is the methodology that structures the remainder of this thesis.
