The evidence for dark matter comes from many independent probes: galactic rotation curves, the motions of galaxies within clusters, gravitational lensing, and the anisotropies of the cosmic microwave background all require a form of matter that outweighs ordinary baryons roughly five to one and interacts with light only through gravity. These probes, however, are all gravitational, and they say little about what dark matter actually is. If it consists of weakly interacting massive particles, it could annihilate or decay into Standard Model states, and gamma rays are especially attractive messengers: undeflected by magnetic fields, they carry the spectral signature of the interaction that produced them. For more than fifteen years the Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT) has surveyed the GeV sky where these signatures are expected, and its measurements are at the core of this thesis.

We argue that progress in indirect dark matter searches now depends on statistical and machine-learning methods capable of recovering signals in noise-dominated environments. The work follows an arc from the Galactic Center outward to the cosmic web, tracking the expected signal into regimes of progressively lower signal-to-noise ratio.

The Galactic Center hosts an excess of GeV photons that may signal dark matter annihilation or an unresolved population of millisecond pulsars (MSPs). To test the pulsar hypothesis we measured the gamma-ray luminosity function of MSPs in Milky Way globular clusters. Had the excess been produced by pulsars with these properties, Fermi-LAT would already have resolved roughly 17 to 37 of them, whereas only three candidates are known — a tension that reignites the debate over the origin of the excess.

We then searched for dark matter subhalos among the Fermi-LAT sources that lack an astrophysical counterpart, building the first generative mixture model of this unassociated population, corrected for prior and covariate shifts. We found no significant subhalo contribution and set 95% confidence upper limits on the annihilation cross section for dark matter masses between 10 GeV and 1 TeV.

Below the detection threshold, we trained a convolutional neural network on about one million synthetic sky maps to reconstruct the source-count distribution dN/dS of extragalactic sources, reaching fluxes a factor of about 50 below the Fermi-LAT threshold. Building on this distribution, we developed a probabilistic cataloging framework that identifies roughly 50% more candidate source directions than the 4FGL-DR3 catalog, released publicly and well suited to cross-correlation studies.

At the largest scales, we forecast the sensitivity of the Cherenkov Telescope Array Observatory to the cross-correlation between gamma-ray anisotropies and low-redshift galaxy catalogs, reaching sensitivities to annihilating and decaying dark matter comparable to those of dwarf-galaxy and galaxy-cluster analyses.

None of these searches returns a confirmed detection, and the particle identity of dark matter remains an open problem. This thesis contributes the statistical tools — population models for sub-threshold sources, inference frameworks robust to the mismatch between simulations and real data, probabilistic catalogs, and cross-correlation analyses — needed to extend the search as next-generation observatories come online.
