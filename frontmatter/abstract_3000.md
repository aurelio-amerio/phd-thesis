The evidence for dark matter comes from many independent probes: galactic rotation curves, galaxy motions in clusters, gravitational lensing, and the cosmic microwave background all require a form of matter that outweighs ordinary baryons roughly five to one and interacts with light only through gravity. Yet all these probes are gravitational, and say little about what dark matter is. If it consists of weakly interacting massive particles, it could annihilate or decay into Standard Model states, and gamma rays are ideal messengers: undeflected by magnetic fields, they carry the spectral signature of their origin. For over fifteen years the Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT) has surveyed the GeV sky; its measurements are at the core of this thesis.

We argue that progress in indirect dark matter searches now depends on statistical and machine-learning methods able to recover signals in noise-dominated environments, and we follow an arc from the Galactic Center out to the cosmic web, into regimes of ever lower signal-to-noise.

The Galactic Center hosts an excess of GeV photons that may signal dark matter annihilation or an unresolved population of millisecond pulsars (MSPs). We measured the gamma-ray luminosity function of MSPs in Milky Way globular clusters: pulsars with these properties would already have produced 17 to 37 resolved sources, whereas only three candidates are known, a tension that reignites the debate over the origin of the excess.

We searched for dark matter subhalos among the Fermi-LAT sources without astrophysical counterparts, building the first generative mixture model of this unassociated population, corrected for prior and covariate shifts. Finding no significant subhalo contribution, we set 95% confidence upper limits on the annihilation cross section for masses between 10 GeV and 1 TeV.

Below the detection threshold, we trained a convolutional neural network on a million synthetic sky maps to reconstruct the source-count distribution dN/dS of extragalactic sources, reaching fluxes fifty times below the catalog threshold. From it we built a probabilistic cataloging framework that identifies roughly 50% more candidate source directions than the 4FGL-DR3 catalog, released publicly for cross-correlation studies.

At the largest scales, we forecast the sensitivity of the Cherenkov Telescope Array Observatory to the cross-correlation between gamma-ray anisotropies and low-redshift galaxy catalogs, with sensitivities to annihilating and decaying dark matter comparable to dwarf-galaxy and cluster analyses.

None of these searches returns a confirmed detection, and the particle identity of dark matter remains an open problem. This thesis contributes statistical tools: population models for sub-threshold sources, inference robust to simulation-data mismatch, probabilistic catalogs, and cross-correlation analyses, to extend the search as next-generation observatories come online.
