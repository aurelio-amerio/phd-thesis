## 1. Motivation

Section 3.3 is the thesis's only dedicated machine-learning section. In its current form it has three weaknesses, all flagged in Review Mode (annotation IDs `jea0uil`, `fu3sqph`, `vhlua9z` on `chapter_03/sections/3.3_ml_astrophysics.tex`):

1. The cost-function paragraph (lines 70–83) is a deep dive on the heteroscedastic Gaussian NLL — a methodology specific to Paper 1. The reviewer asks that this section be reframed as a *general* introduction to cost functions, with the heteroscedastic-NLL formalism moved into Chapter 6 (where it is actually used) and references modernised to acknowledge that contemporary SBI does not generally rely on this approach.
2. The regularization paragraph (lines 87–90) treats concrete dropout as if it were the regularization method. The reviewer asks that this be reframed: dropout is one tool among several (batch normalisation, weight decay, etc.), and modern practice typically combines multiple techniques rather than relying on dropout alone.
3. The density-estimation subsection (line 97 onward) is too broadly scoped. Density estimation today is a wide landscape (KDE, mixture models, normalising flows, score-based diffusion, flow matching, MCMC, etc.); the section should explicitly state that here we focus on the classical non-parametric methods actually used in Chapter 5, while acknowledging the broader landscape briefly.

In addition, the author wants:

- The CNN content **expanded**, not trimmed, because §3.3 is the thesis's only dedicated ML section and currently does not give CNNs the breath they deserve. The spherical-CNN content (DeepSphere / NNHealpix / `map2patch` / HEAL-SWIN) is good and should stay.
- A clear conceptual grounding for *why* CNNs are emphasised: image-domain analyses of telescope data are one viable paradigm (Chapter 6); summary-statistic analyses on hand-crafted features are another (Chapter 5). The section should make this two-paradigm framing explicit so that the CNN emphasis feels motivated. **Important framing constraint** (saved as memory `feedback-no-overstatement`): do *not* claim CNNs / computer-vision are "the dominant" or "predominant" paradigm — frame as one of two complementary approaches used in this thesis, not as the field-wide default.
- A pedagogical CNN figure ("the usual graphic that shows how a filter is applied in strides/windows to a figure") and a spherical-convolution illustration (Fig. 2 from `Krachmalnicoff:2019`, arXiv:1902.04083). Two separate figures, not a side-by-side panel.

The target length is 5–6 pages including 1–2 figures. ML is a *means* to a physical goal in this thesis, so the section must be substantive without becoming a textbook.

## 3. Framing thread (section opener)

The opening paragraphs of §3.3 will state — accurately, without overclaiming — that this thesis uses two broad ML paradigms:

- **Image-domain analyses**, where the network ingests pixel arrays directly (telescope images, sky maps). Computer-vision architectures, especially CNNs, are a natural choice because the inductive biases of convolution (locality, weight sharing, translation equivariance) match how astrophysical signals appear on detectors. Chapter 6 (Paper 1) is in this category.
- **Summary-statistic analyses**, where each object is first reduced to a low-dimensional feature vector (spectral parameters, variability indices, fluxes, catalog quantities) and ML operates on those features. Chapter 5 (Paper 4) is in this category.

Both are widely used; the choice depends on data type and scientific question. This framing motivates the structure of the section: §3.3.3 covers CNNs (and their spherical extensions, since sky maps live on $S^2$); §3.3.4 covers density-estimation tools for population-level inference on feature vectors. The opening is explicit that the section is a primer, not a survey — references are provided for readers who want more.

### 3.3.2 Neural networks (~1.5 p)

Five short paragraphs.

1. **Network architecture.** Keep current opening (layer composition, ReLU, universal approximation). Add one sentence acknowledging other activations (sigmoid, tanh, GELU) as historical / modern alternatives.
2. **Training: backpropagation and gradient descent.** New paragraph. Backprop as the chain-rule procedure that yields gradients of the loss with respect to every weight; mini-batch SGD and modern variants (Adam, AdamW) named without derivation; one sentence on depth + scale + GPU compute as the empirical engine of modern deep learning.
3. **Loss functions (general).** Reframed per reviewer. MSE for regression, cross-entropy for classification, negative log-likelihood as the unifying probabilistic view. **One sentence** noting that the variance itself can be parameterised when noise levels vary across the data (heteroscedastic NLL), with a citation and a forward-pointer to the paper subsection in Ch 6 (`sec:bayesian-error`) for the actual formalism — **no equation in §3.3.**
4. **Uncertainty estimation.** Reframed and concise, no name-dropping (per author direction).
    > Beyond a point estimate, predictions carry two distinct sources of error: *aleatoric* uncertainty, intrinsic to the data (irreducible noise that more training data cannot remove), and *epistemic* uncertainty, reflecting the model's ignorance about which parameter values best describe the data (reducible in the limit of infinite data and capacity). Bayesian neural networks aim to recover both jointly by treating the network weights as random variables rather than fixed parameters, in the same spirit as the simulation-based inference framework of §3.2. Chapter 6 presents one practical realisation of this idea, in which MC dropout and a heteroscedastic Gaussian likelihood are combined to produce per-output mean and variance estimates; the technical formalism is detailed there (§\ref{sec:bayesian-error}).
   No survey of VI / deep ensembles / Laplace.
5. **Regularization (general).** Reframed per reviewer. Overparameterized models can memorise the training set; regularization is what keeps them honest. Family of complementary techniques *typically used in combination*: weight decay (L2), dropout (and its self-tuning concrete variant), batch / layer normalisation, data augmentation, early stopping. Explicit framing: not "dropout is the answer." Chapter 6's specific recipe (concrete dropout) is presented as one valid choice.

- New (to verify before insertion): Goodfellow-Bengio-Courville 2016 *Deep Learning*; LeCun-Bengio-Hinton 2015 Nature review; Murphy 2022 *Probabilistic Machine Learning*; Kendall-Gal 2017 (aleatoric/epistemic); Ioffe-Szegedy 2015 (batch norm); Loshchilov-Hutter 2019 (AdamW). **Citation-handling rule applies to all of these (§5 below).**

### 3.3.3 Convolutional neural networks (~2.5 p — the centerpiece)

Eight content paragraphs plus two figures.

1. **Why CNNs for image-like astrophysical data.** Completes the two-paradigm thread from the section intro: for image-domain analyses, the input is a pixel grid (or a HEALPix sky map) and convolutional architectures exploit that spatial structure directly. Names a few canonical astrophysical-vision applications briefly (galaxy-morphology classification, strong-lensing detection, transient identification), explicitly *not* claiming CNNs are "the" workhorse — just as orienting examples.
2. **The convolution operation.** What a convolutional layer computes: learnable kernel sliding over the input producing a feature map; weight sharing → parameter count independent of input size; translation equivariance as a mathematical consequence. One short equation defining discrete 2D convolution. This paragraph is what Fig A illustrates.
3. **Hierarchy and pooling.** Stacking convolutions builds receptive fields; deeper layers encode progressively more abstract patterns. Pooling (max / average) downsamples and provides approximate translation *invariance* at the cost of spatial resolution.
4. **[Fig A]** Generic CNN architecture cartoon — input image → conv + activation → pool → conv + activation → pool → fully-connected → output, with feature maps shrinking spatially and growing in channel depth. **Source:** adapted from a published deep-learning reference with attribution. **Candidate sources** (to evaluate during drafting, by visual clarity and reuse licensing): LeCun-Bengio-Hinton 2015 Nature Fig. 2; Goodfellow-Bengio-Courville Fig. 9.7; Khan et al. 2020 *Artificial Intelligence Review* CNN survey. Final choice noted in the implementation plan.
5. **Modern CNN architectures (brief).** Two sentences, per author direction — not a history lesson:
    > Modern CNNs build on a small number of architectural innovations that consistently improve performance: most prominently VGG-style stacks of small convolutions, and the residual connections introduced by ResNet, which enabled training of networks hundreds of layers deep. Contemporary computer-vision architectures often combine these convolutional building blocks with attention mechanisms borrowed from transformers, as in vision transformers and hybrid models.
6. **The spherical complication.** Sky maps live on $S^2$, not on $\mathbb{R}^2$. Standard CNNs implicitly assume Euclidean translation symmetry; the sphere has rotational symmetry instead, and any flat parametrisation introduces distortion that breaks weight sharing. This motivates the strategies that follow.
7. **Three strategies for spherical convolution.** Consolidated from the current text. Spectral / graph-based (DeepSphere on HEALPix), projective (icosahedral tiling), pixel-based (NNHealpix, `map2patch`, HEAL-SWIN). Keep most of the current prose but tighten the `map2patch` discussion — the full treatment lives in Chapter 6, so here only signal what it does (12 base-pixel tiling → standard 2D conv on each patch) and why it was developed (speed + topology preservation).
8. **[Fig B]** Fig. 2 from `Krachmalnicoff:2019` (arXiv:1902.04083) — the pixel-based NNHealpix strategy on the HEALPix sphere. Reproduced with attribution. Bib key already exists in `bibliography.bib`.
9. **Application preview.** Chapter 6 uses EfficientNet V2M + `map2patch` + concrete dropout + heteroscedastic NLL; full details deferred, with explicit forward-link to `sec:bayesian-error` for the Gal formalism. *EfficientNet V2M is named only here as a forward-pointer — the architectural choice is justified in Chapter 6, not in §3.3.*

### 3.3.4 Density estimation (~1.5 p)

Five short paragraphs.

1. **Landscape paragraph (new, per reviewer).** Density estimation is a broad problem with several modern approaches. As §3.2 introduced, simulation-based inference relies on neural density estimators — normalising flows (the typical choice for low-to-moderate dimensions, exact log-likelihood and single-pass sampling), score-based diffusion models (more flexible architecturally but with multi-step sampling), and the more recent flow-matching paradigm — which excel when the data are high-dimensional or the underlying distribution is highly complex. Classical non-parametric methods — kernel density estimation, mixture models with EM — remain valuable in the complementary regime of low-dimensional, well-defined feature spaces, where neural surrogates are not necessary and the curse of dimensionality is not the binding constraint. Chapter 5 sits in this regime, and the rest of this subsection introduces the classical tools used there.
2. **Kernel Density Estimation.** Largely current text, slightly tightened. Definition (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kde}), Gaussian kernel, bandwidth as the bias-variance trade-off knob, cross-validation for bandwidth selection. Class-conditional densities $p_\mathrm{assoc}(\mathbf{x}|k)$ as building blocks for Ch 5.
3. **Mixture models.** Current text. Equation \eqref{eq:mixture_model}, $\pi_k$ as mixing weights, $p(\mathbf{x}|k)$ as class-conditional densities.
4. **EM algorithm.** Current text. E-step (posterior class responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$); M-step (closed-form weight update $\pi_k^\mathrm{new} = N^{-1}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}$).
5. **Application preview.** Ch 5 uses this framework combined with the covariate-shift correction of §3.4, with class prevalences fit via EM following Saerens et al. — full details deferred.

**References used / added here (all to be verified):**
- Existing: `bishop`, `Dempster:1977`, `10.1162/089976602753284446_Saerens`.
- New (to verify): Papamakarios et al. 2021 *Normalising Flows for Probabilistic Modeling and Inference* (JMLR); Cranmer-Brehmer-Louppe 2020 SBI review (likely already in bib — confirm); Lipman et al. 2023 *Flow Matching for Generative Modeling*; Song et al. 2021 *Score-based Generative Modeling Through SDEs* (for diffusion); a textbook for classical KDE (Silverman 1986 or Scott 1992 — pick one).

## 5. Citation-handling rule (cross-section)

Every new reference introduced in §3.3 must be verified against InspireHEP (or arXiv for sources outside HEP) and its bib key confirmed against `bibliography.bib` before insertion. **No hallucinated keys.** The implementation plan must explicitly schedule a citation-verification pass *before* drafting prose. The relevant skills serve different stages and should not be conflated:

- **Pre-flight (before drafting):** the `paper_lookup` skill for metadata + BibTeX of a specific paper. When more context than the abstract is needed to write a paragraph honestly, the same skill can pull the arXiv PDF, which can then be read directly. This is the right tool for any new reference under consideration.
- **During drafting:** the `fixref` skill for resolving placeholder citations or missing bibliography entries that surface while writing.
- **Post-drafting QA only:** the `citecheck` skill — and only after the section is *written* — to verify that inserted `\cite{...}` references actually fit the surrounding narrative. `citecheck` is not part of planning or pre-flight; it scores pertinence against InspireHEP / arXiv abstracts on finished prose.

If a paper is missing from `bibliography.bib`, the entry is added during drafting using the BibTeX returned by InspireHEP / arXiv, *not* by guessing a key from the author name and year.
