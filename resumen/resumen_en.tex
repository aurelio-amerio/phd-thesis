\section*{Preface}
The evidence for dark matter is overwhelming, and yet it tells us almost nothing about what dark matter is made of. Galactic rotation curves stay flat where the visible mass predicts a decline; galaxies inside clusters move too fast to remain bound by the luminous matter alone; light from distant sources is bent by mass we cannot see; and the pattern of anisotropies in the cosmic microwave background is reproduced only when a non-luminous component is added to the budget. Each of these measurements, drawn from a different scale and a different epoch, points to the same conclusion: a form of matter that outweighs ordinary baryons by roughly a factor of five and interacts with light only through gravity. Within the standard cosmological model this component accounts for about 85\% of the matter density, cold and non-baryonic. The agreement across independent probes is remarkable, but it is also frustrating, because every one of these probes is gravitational. They tell us how much dark matter there is and where it sits; they say nothing about its particle nature. Proposed candidates span nearly ninety orders of magnitude in mass, from ultralight axions to primordial black holes, and the data alone single out none of them.

Among these candidates, weakly interacting massive particles (WIMPs) have long held a privileged place. A stable particle with an electroweak-scale mass and coupling, produced thermally in the hot early Universe, freezes out with a relic abundance that lands naturally near the observed dark matter density, a coincidence known as the WIMP miracle. The annihilation process that fixes this abundance does not switch off once the Universe cools: wherever dark matter piles up densely today, WIMPs should continue to annihilate, converting their rest mass into Standard Model particles such as gamma rays, neutrinos, positrons, and antiprotons. If dark matter is instead unstable, it may decay into the same states. Of the messengers this produces, gamma rays are the most informative. They travel undeflected by magnetic fields, so they point back to where they were made, and they carry the spectral fingerprint of the interaction that produced them. A gamma-ray signal from a region of concentrated dark matter would therefore be one of the cleanest handles we have on the particle behind the gravitational anomalies.

For more than fifteen years the Large Area Telescope (LAT) on board the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope has surveyed the GeV sky where such signatures are expected, and its measurements sit at the core of this thesis. That long exposure has changed the character of the problem. The brightest targets have already been studied at length, and the signals that remain are faint, blended with astrophysical foregrounds, or hidden below the detection threshold. Standard methods classify the brightest objects and map the prominent diffuse components reliably, but they struggle with overlapping populations and with instrumental limits such as finite angular resolution and the detection threshold itself. Setting a threshold and counting what rises above it is a natural first move, yet it discards most of the information: a large fraction of the emitting population is left unresolved, unclassified, or simply unseen.

We argue in this thesis that progress in indirect dark matter searches now depends on statistical and machine-learning methods able to recover signals in these noise-dominated regimes, where simple thresholding fails. The work follows an arc from the Galactic Center outward to the cosmic web, tracking the expected signal into regimes of steadily lower signal-to-noise. As the signal weakens, the methodology has to grow more sophisticated to keep pace, and we draw in turn on traditional statistical inference, generative modeling, implicit-likelihood techniques, and deep learning, applied both to Fermi-LAT data and to forecasts for future instruments.

Five studies make up this progression. We begin with a measurement of the gamma-ray luminosity function of millisecond pulsars (MSPs) in Milky Way globular clusters, used to test whether an unresolved pulsar population can account for the Galactic Center Excess. We then search for dark matter subhalos among the unassociated Fermi-LAT sources, combining a generative mixture model with quantification learning. Moving below the catalog threshold, we reconstruct the source-count distribution of faint extragalactic sources with a deep neural network, and we build a probabilistic cataloging framework that extends gamma-ray catalogs into the sub-threshold regime, released publicly as the gamma-ray Photon Count Statistics (gPCS) catalog, a Python package accompanied by a FITS table. Finally, we forecast the sensitivity of the Cherenkov Telescope Array Observatory to the cross-correlation between gamma rays and galaxy catalogs, carrying the search out to the largest scales and the weakest expected signals.

\section*{The dark matter problem}
The case for dark matter rests on a chain of independent observations assembled over nearly a century. The first quantitative hint came in 1933, when Zwicky applied the virial theorem to the radial velocities of galaxies in the Coma Cluster and inferred a mass-to-light ratio of order 400, far larger than the luminous matter could supply; he named the missing component \emph{dunkle Materie} \cite{Zwicky:1933}. The discrepancy reappeared on galactic scales when Rubin and Ford measured the first high-precision optical rotation curve of M31 in 1970 and found no Keplerian decline at large radii \cite{Rubin:1970zza}. Later 21-cm observations extended these rotation curves to the largest measurable radii in hundreds of systems, where the rotation velocity stays flat; a flat curve implies an enclosed mass growing linearly with radius, $M(r) \propto r$, and therefore an extended halo whose density falls as $\rho \propto r^{-2}$ at large radii. Gravitational lensing supplied a cleaner argument still: in the Bullet Cluster the weak-lensing mass reconstruction is spatially offset from the X-ray-emitting gas and coincides instead with the effectively collisionless galaxies \cite{Clowe:2006eq}, as expected if most of the mass is dark and nearly collisionless. On cosmological scales the cosmic microwave background completes the picture, since a universe without cold dark matter would produce a strongly suppressed third acoustic peak, in conflict with the observed spectrum.

These measurements agree not only that dark matter exists but on how much of it there is. The Planck 2018 analysis yields $\Omega_c h^2 = 0.1200 \pm 0.0012$ for the cold dark matter density \cite{Aghanim:2018eyx}, making dark matter roughly five times more abundant than ordinary baryonic matter. Any viable candidate must reproduce this relic density while remaining consistent with the dynamical and lensing evidence.

A WIMP offers a natural way to do so: a particle with a mass in the GeV--TeV range and weak-scale couplings to the Standard Model. If such a particle was in thermal equilibrium in the hot early universe and froze out as the plasma cooled, its surviving abundance is set by the rate at which it annihilated. For $s$-wave annihilation this relic abundance takes the compact form
\be
\frac{\Omega_\chi h^2}{0.12} \approx \frac{2.2 \times 10^{-26}~\mathrm{cm^3/s}}{\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel}\rangle},
\ee
where the numerator is the canonical thermal cross section, $\langle \sigma v\rangle \approx 2.2 \times 10^{-26}~\mathrm{cm^3/s}$ \cite{Steigman:2012nb, Cirelli:2024ssz}. What makes the hypothesis compelling is a coincidence: a cross section of roughly weak strength, $\langle \sigma v\rangle \sim \alpha^2/m_\chi^2$ with $\alpha \sim 0.03$, returns the observed abundance for a mass of a few hundred GeV to a few TeV, precisely the electroweak scale. This alignment between a cosmological measurement and a particle-physics scale is the so-called WIMP miracle.

The same coupling that governs dark matter annihilation in the early universe also mediates scattering off nuclei in underground detectors and pair-production at particle colliders, which opens three complementary experimental strategies. Direct detection searches for the nuclear recoil imparted when a dark matter particle crosses a deep-underground detector. Collider searches look for the missing transverse energy carried away when dark matter is produced at the Large Hadron Collider. Indirect detection, the approach followed throughout this thesis, targets the annihilation or decay products of dark matter in astrophysical environments where its density is high.

For annihilation into gamma rays, the observed differential flux factorizes cleanly into a particle-physics term and an astrophysical term,
\be
\frac{d\Phi_\gamma}{dE\, d\Omega} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{4\pi} \frac{\langle \sigma v_\text{rel} \rangle}{2 m_\chi^2} \frac{dN_\gamma}{dE}}_{\text{Particle Physics}} \quad \times \underbrace{J(\psi)}_{\text{Astrophysics}},
\ee
where the astrophysical factor is the line-of-sight integral of the squared density,
\be
J(\psi) = \int_\text{l.o.s.} \rho^2\bigl(r(s, \psi)\bigr) \, ds.
\ee
The particle-physics factor holds the mass $m_\chi$, the cross section $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel}\rangle$ (or, for decay, the rate $\Gamma$), and the spectral shape $dN_\gamma/dE$: it encodes the model we wish to test \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz, Hooper:2024}. The astrophysical factor $J$ encodes only the distribution of dark matter along the line of sight. This separation is what makes indirect searches tractable, because a non-detection in a given direction translates directly into an upper limit on $\langle \sigma v_\mathrm{rel}\rangle$ once $J$ is known. It also fixes where the leading uncertainty lies: $J$ is quadratic in the density profile, and the profile is the quantity we measure least well, so the astrophysics dominates the systematic budget of any annihilation search. (For decaying dark matter the flux instead scales with $\Gamma/m_\chi$ and a $D$-factor linear in $\rho$, which softens this sensitivity to the profile.)

Because the expected signal scales with the density integral, $J$ orders the targets of any annihilation search, and with them the structure of this thesis. The Galactic Center has the largest J-factor of any astronomical source, owing to its proximity ($\sim 8$~kpc) and the steep expected rise of the dark matter density at small radii; it also carries the most severe astrophysical contamination, from bright diffuse emission and a crowded field of point sources. Dwarf spheroidal galaxies and dark subhalos sit lower in $J$ but far higher in cleanliness: dwarfs are among the most dark-matter-dominated systems known, and $\Lambda$CDM predicts many more gravitationally bound subhalos than the roughly 50 known satellite galaxies, most of them entirely dark and expected to appear in the Fermi-LAT data as unassociated point sources with no counterpart at other wavelengths \cite{Springel:2008cc}. At the largest scales lies the extragalactic web, whose dark matter contribution is buried in the unresolved gamma-ray background that remains once resolved sources and Galactic diffuse emission are subtracted; there the signal is expected to be subdominant in total intensity and must be extracted with anisotropy and cross-correlation techniques. The chapters that follow descend this hierarchy in order: the Galactic Center first, where the expected signal is brightest, then dark subhalos, then the faint sub-threshold source population, and finally the cosmic web, at the weakest signal of all.
