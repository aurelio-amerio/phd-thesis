\chapter*{Conclusions and Future Directions}
\label{chap:outlook}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Conclusions and Future Directions}
\markboth{Conclusions and Future Directions}{Conclusions and Future Directions}

The particle nature of dark matter remains unknown, even as its
gravitational imprint is written across every scale of the Universe. Gamma
rays are among the cleanest messengers we have to look for the non-gravitational
signatures of that dark sector, since an annihilation or decay signal would
add photons to a sky we probe extensively.
The difficulty is that any such signal would be buried in astrophysical
backgrounds that are bright, structured, and imperfectly known, so the dark
matter component we are after is faint and easily mimicked.
This thesis followed a common thread through that problem: recovering signals in
noise-dominated regimes, where simple thresholding fails, requires statistical
and machine-learning methods matched to each regime. We organized the work along
that thread, tracking the expected signal from the Galactic Center Excess into
regimes of progressively lower signal-to-noise ratio, down to the cross-correlation forecasts for
the next generation of instruments. To this end, we drew on traditional
statistical inference, generative modeling, simulation-based inference, and deep
learning.

Along the way we measured the gamma-ray luminosity function of millisecond
pulsars in Milky Way globular clusters with 15.8 years of Fermi-LAT data, and
found that a pulsar interpretation of the Galactic Center Excess should already
have produced resolved sources that are not observed~\cite{Amerio:2024qor}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:4}). We built what is, to our knowledge, the first
probabilistic mixture model of the high-latitude unassociated Fermi-LAT
sources, found no significant dark matter subhalo population, and set $95\%$
confidence upper limits on $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ in the $b\bar{b}$ channel
for dark matter masses between $10$~GeV and $1$~TeV~\cite{Amerio:2025fhz}
(Chapter~\ref{ch:5}). We trained a convolutional network on roughly $10^{6}$
synthetic maps to reconstruct the extragalactic source-count distribution
\dnds down to fluxes a factor of fifty below the catalog
threshold~\cite{Amerio:2023uet} (Chapter~\ref{ch:6}), and used the
reconstructed \dnds as a generative model to build the public gPCS
(gamma-ray Photon Count Statistics) probabilistic catalog, which identifies
about $50\%$ more candidate source directions than 4FGL-DR3
alone~\cite{Amerio:2023rjn} (Chapter~\ref{ch:7}). Finally, we forecast that the
cross-correlation of the anisotropic gamma-ray sky with low-redshift galaxy
catalogs will let CTAO probe $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ down to
$\sim 10^{-23}\,\mathrm{cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}$ with modest
exposure~\cite{Pinetti:2025hgd} (Chapter~\ref{ch:8}).

No search in this thesis ended in a dark matter detection. Each, however, sharpened the way
the question is asked, and each leaves a natural set of next steps. In light of
these findings, several directions can be pursued, one per research line
developed in this thesis.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The millisecond pulsar hypothesis for the Galactic Center
    Excess:} Our constraint is conditional: if the pulsars in the Inner Galaxy
    share the luminosity function we measured in surviving globular clusters,
    Fermi-LAT should already have resolved $17$--$37$ of them, whereas only
    three candidates are known~\cite{Amerio:2024qor}. This tension converges
    with independent analyses of the excess's energy
    distribution~\cite{List:2025qbx}. The main open uncertainty is how the
    pulsars reached the Inner Galaxy in the first place. If they arrived with
    globular clusters that spiraled in through dynamical friction and were
    tidally disrupted~\cite{Gnedin:2013cda,Brandt:2015ula,Fragione:2017rsp},
    they might be older than the pulsars in surviving clusters, whose dense
    cores continue to recycle new, bright millisecond pulsars today. An older
    population could be fainter, but exactly how much this would affect the
    constraints we set remains an open question. Progress will require a better
    understanding of this formation and delivery history, along with deeper
    radio and multi-wavelength characterization of the unassociated Fermi-LAT
    sources that could hide the missing pulsars.

    \item \textbf{Dark matter subhalos among unassociated sources:} We
    constrained the subhalo contribution to the unassociated sources by maximum
    likelihood, using quantification learning to handle the covariate and prior
    shift between the associated sources used for training and the unassociated
    targets, and we found no significant excess~\cite{Amerio:2025fhz}. A natural extension is to velocity-dependent
    annihilation, such as Sommerfeld enhancement~\cite{ArkaniHamed:2008qn}:
    the nearest small subhalos have velocity dispersions of a few
    $\mathrm{km\,s^{-1}}$~\cite{2024MNRAS.530.2496A}, which could raise the
    expected signal by a factor of a few relative to dwarf galaxies.
    The method itself is not tied to
    gamma-ray astronomy: it applies to anomaly searches in any unlabeled
    dataset that exhibits covariate and prior shift. Closer to home, the same
    framework could be applied to the unassociated sources near the Galactic
    plane, to assess how many of them are consistent with a millisecond-pulsar
    origin.

    \item \textbf{The source-count distribution below threshold:} The network
    recovered a \dnds{} compatible with an $S^{-2}$ scaling down to fluxes
    roughly fifty times fainter than the catalog limit
    \cite{Amerio:2023uet}. Extending the analysis to multiple energy bins would
    give an energy-dependent \dnds{} and access to energy correlations,
    sharpening the overall sensitivity of the method. It might also separate source
    classes with distinct spectral shapes and expose features in the \dnds{}
    that no known astrophysical population produces --- a possible imprint of
    dark matter, complementary to the cross-correlation searches of
    Chapter~\ref{ch:8}.

    \item \textbf{Probabilistic cataloging of the gamma-ray sky:} Using the
    reconstructed \dnds{} as a generative model, gPCS compares synthetic and
    real skies pixel by pixel and flags candidate source directions
    \cite{Amerio:2023rjn}. Its main application is in cross-correlation studies
    with galaxy catalogs, along two routes. Cross-matching the candidate
    directions with the positions of cataloged galaxies can promote some of
    them into bona fide sources, an identification effort that machine-learning
    classification of unassociated objects can support~\cite{Bhat:2022}.
    Alternatively, the catalog can be used as a gamma-ray tracer in
    cross-correlations with galaxy tracers of the large-scale structure, in the
    spirit of Chapter~\ref{ch:8}: there, the statistical reach of a $50\%$
    larger sample outweighs a sub-leading fraction of spurious directions, and
    the resulting measurement could be more sensitive, for example, to a dark
    matter component.

    \item \textbf{Cross-correlations with the large-scale structure:} Our
    forecast showed that cross-correlating the gamma-ray anisotropies with the
    2MASS galaxy distribution lets CTAO reach $\langle\sigma v\rangle \sim
    10^{-23}\,\mathrm{cm^{3}\,s^{-1}}$ and decay lifetimes of order
    $10^{27}\,\mathrm{s}$ with about $50$~h of effective exposure, competitive
    with dwarf and cluster analyses and roughly five times stronger than the
    auto-correlation~\cite{Pinetti:2025hgd}. Deeper and more densely sampled
    catalogs such as Euclid~\cite{2024arXiv240513491E} would tighten the
    tomographic reconstruction, as would adding other large-scale-structure
    tracers such as weak lensing. Finally, two steps lie ahead: updating these
    forecasts as the construction of CTAO progresses and its survey strategy is
    finalized, and carrying out the cross-correlation measurement itself once
    real data become available.
\end{itemize}

Taken together, the five analyses tell a consistent story. No dark matter
signal was found, which is itself informative: the parameter space where a
gamma-ray signal would already have been obvious is shrinking. Progress in
indirect detection rests on improving the instruments, the exposure they
accumulate, and the statistical machinery that reads the resulting data --- and
none can substitute for the others. The work presented here contributed to the
third.
We developed population-level models for source classes we cannot resolve one
by one, inference schemes that remain reliable when the training sample does not
perfectly match the data, probabilistic catalogs that keep track of what we do
not know, and forecasts that show where the next instruments will be most
sensitive.

These methods are not only applicable to the problems that motivated them. The
neural reconstruction of source-count distributions, the quantification-learning
treatment in the presence of dataset shift, the probabilistic cataloging of
sub-threshold sources, and the cross-correlation forecasting pipeline all carry
over to other datasets and other messengers,
and they will matter more as the Fermi-LAT exposure keeps growing and as CTAO
comes online. The particle nature of dark matter is still an open question. The
tools we have to interrogate it, though, are considerably sharper than when this
work began, and that is the ground on which a future detection, or the next
exclusion, will be built.
