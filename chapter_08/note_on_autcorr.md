**To "assign a redshift" simply means to determine the distance to the source of the emission.** 

In an expanding universe, the physical wavelength of light emitted by a distant object is stretched out as it travels through space, a phenomenon known as cosmological redshift. Because this stretching increases the further the light has to travel, astronomers use redshift as a direct measure of distance and time. 

Here is a breakdown of exactly what this means in the context of gamma-ray fluctuations and why it is a crucial piece of the puzzle:

**The Problem: Gamma-Ray Maps are "Flat"**
When telescopes observe the gamma-ray sky, they record the arrival direction and energy of photons, creating a 2D map of the sky. A "fluctuation" in this map—a particularly clumpy or bright patch—represents the total, integrated emission accumulated along the entire line of sight, capturing everything from the local universe out to vast cosmological distances. 

Unlike optical observations of galaxies, where astronomers can look at specific atomic spectral lines to measure a precise redshift, the continuous spectrum of the gamma-ray background lacks these built-in "distance tags." Therefore, looking at the gamma-ray map in isolation (auto-correlation) only shows you that there are clumps, but leaves you completely blind as to whether those clumps are coming from faint sources in our cosmic backyard or extremely bright sources billions of light-years away. 

**The Solution: Cross-Correlation**
This is why the phrase highlights that auto-correlation "cannot assign a redshift." To figure out the distance of the gamma-ray sources, researchers must cross-correlate the 2D gamma-ray map with a 3D tracer of large-scale structure, such as a galaxy redshift survey. 

Because we *do* know the precise redshifts (distances) of the galaxies in these catalogs, we can slice the galaxy data into specific distance bins. By overlaying the 2D gamma-ray map with these individual 3D distance slices, researchers can see exactly which galaxy distances overlap with the gamma-ray clumps. If the gamma-ray fluctuations strongly correlate with a slice of galaxies at a specific redshift, researchers can confidently **assign that redshift** to the gamma-ray emission. 

**Why it matters**
Having this distance information is essential because different types of gamma-ray sources—like star-forming galaxies, distant blazars, or annihilating Dark Matter halos—have different distributions that peak at different times in cosmic history. By assigning a redshift to the fluctuations, astronomers can peel apart the layers of the gamma-ray sky to identify exactly what is producing the light.
