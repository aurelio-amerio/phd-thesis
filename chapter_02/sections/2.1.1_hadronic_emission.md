### 2.1.1 Hadronic Emission

High-energy cosmic-ray protons and heavier nuclei interact with the interstellar medium through the strong nuclear force, primarily colliding with ambient hydrogen gas. These inelastic proton-proton collisions produce secondary particles, most notably pions:
$$
p + p \to \pi^0 + \pi^{\pm} + X.
$$
The reaction is subject to a strict kinematic threshold. A cosmic-ray proton colliding with a stationary target proton must have a total energy of at least $E_p > m_p + 2m_\pi + m_\pi^2 / 2m_p \approx 1.2$ GeV in order to produce one or more pions \cite{Hooper_2024}. Once this threshold is crossed, the total inelastic cross section increases only mildly with energy, rising from $\sigma_\text{inel}^{pp} \approx 30$--$40$ mb at $E_p \lesssim 10$ GeV to $\sigma_\text{inel}^{pp} \approx 100$ mb near $E_p \sim 1$ EeV \cite{Hooper_2024}. Because a significant fraction of the proton's kinetic energy is transferred in each collision (inelasticity $K_{pp} \sim 0.5$), only a few successive interactions suffice to extract most of the cosmic ray's original energy \cite{Dermer_2009}.

Modeling the pion production spectrum accurately requires combining different theoretical frameworks across energy regimes. At proton kinetic energies below approximately 3 GeV, pion production is dominated by the excitation of the $\Delta_{3/2}(1232)$ baryonic resonance. In this isobaric model, the resonance is produced collinearly with the incident proton in the center-of-momentum frame and subsequently decays isotropically in its rest frame, yielding a pion spectrum that is computed by integrating over the resonance mass distribution (described by a Breit-Wigner profile) \cite{Dermer_2009}. Above 7 GeV, the number of accessible decay channels becomes too large for exclusive descriptions, and an inclusive scaling model based on Feynman scaling is adopted instead \cite{Dermer_2009}. A linear interpolation smoothly joins the two formalisms in the intermediate 3--7 GeV range. More recently, Kafexhiu et al.\ provided a comprehensive, energy-dependent parametrization of the $pp \to \pi^0$ inclusive cross section that is widely adopted in current gamma-ray analyses \cite{Kafexhiu_2014}.

While charged pions ($\pi^\pm$) decay into muons and neutrinos, neutral pions decay electromagnetically with a mean lifetime of only $\tau_{\pi^0} \approx 8.4 \times 10^{-17}$ s:
$$
\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma.
$$
In the rest frame of the $\pi^0$, this decay produces two photons of equal energy $E_\gamma = m_{\pi^0}/2 \approx 67.5$ MeV. In the laboratory frame, however, a pion moving with energy $E_\pi$ generates a gamma-ray spectrum that is flat between the kinematic bounds $(E_\pi - \sqrt{E_\pi^2 - m_\pi^2})/2$ and $(E_\pi + \sqrt{E_\pi^2 - m_\pi^2})/2$, and vanishes outside this interval \cite{Hooper_2024}. This flat distribution has a remarkable consequence: regardless of the spectral shape of the parent pion population, the resulting gamma-ray spectrum---when plotted as $E^2 \, dN/dE$---always peaks at $m_{\pi^0}/2 \approx 67.5$ MeV. This universal spectral feature, known as the \emph{pion bump}, is the definitive spectral signature of hadronic cosmic-ray interactions with gas and has been conclusively observed in both supernova remnants and the Galactic Plane emission \cite{Ackermann:2013wqa}.

Hadronic emission dominates the Galactic Diffuse Emission along the Galactic Plane, where the column density of interstellar gas is highest. As a result, it constitutes the primary astrophysical background for any dark matter search targeting the inner Galaxy. Mismodeling the pion-decay component introduces systematic errors that directly impact the extraction of the Galactic Center Excess and the identification of dark matter subhalos. While hadronic emission dominates wherever gas is abundant, leptonic processes---inverse Compton scattering, bremsstrahlung, and synchrotron radiation---provide the complementary diffuse components, particularly at high Galactic latitudes.
