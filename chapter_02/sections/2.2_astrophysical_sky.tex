\section{The Astrophysical Gamma-Ray Sky}
\label{sec:astro_sky}

The high-energy sky observed by the Fermi Large Area Telescope (Fermi-LAT) is a complex superposition of energetic processes spanning our local Galactic neighborhood and the broader universe.
Phenomenologically, this emission falls into two domains.
The Galactic sky is dominated by the diffuse foreground of cosmic-ray interactions and populated by energetic point sources, most notably pulsars.
The extragalactic sky, in contrast, is characterized by an isotropic background arising primarily from unresolved active galactic nuclei and star-forming galaxies.
A detailed understanding of the astrophysical processes behind these conventional emitters is necessary for dark matter indirect detection, where every ordinary astrophysical signal is a potential background.
Isolating any dark matter contribution therefore requires a thorough characterization of the astrophysical gamma-ray sky.

\subsection{Galactic Sources}
\label{sec:galactic_sources}

The gamma-ray emission from the Milky Way comprises two broad components: a diffuse foreground and a diverse population of individual point sources.
The Galactic Diffuse Emission (GDE) dominates the observed sky, accounting for approximately 80\% of all photons detected by the Fermi-LAT \cite{DGRB-review}.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:production_mechanisms}, this emission arises from cosmic-ray interactions with the interstellar medium and radiation fields: hadronic pion production traces the distribution of interstellar gas along the Galactic plane, while inverse-Compton scattering off starlight and the CMB produces a more extended high-latitude component.
To isolate other astrophysical signals, the standard approach relies on template fitting, in which the spatial and spectral distribution of the GDE is modeled using cosmic-ray propagation codes such as GALPROP \cite{Ackermann:2014usa, DGRB-review}.
The Fermi-LAT sky model decomposes the observed counts into a linear combination of these GDE templates, individually resolved point sources, and a residual isotropic component.
However, this decomposition depends on the assumed interstellar emission model (IEM), and the use of different IEM templates leads to systematic differences in the extracted residuals.
Unmodeled gas structures, uncertainties in the cosmic-ray source distribution, and poorly constrained radiation fields all contribute to making the GDE template the largest source of systematic uncertainty in Fermi-LAT analyses.
This mismodeling directly induces dataset shift when applying simulation-trained algorithms to real data, a challenge addressed in Chapter~5.

Beyond the diffuse foreground, the Galactic plane is populated by individual energetic sources, with pulsars being the most prominent gamma-ray emitters.
Young pulsars are rapidly spinning neutron stars with large surface magnetic fields ($10^{11}$--$10^{13}$~G), emitting gamma rays primarily through curvature radiation in their magnetospheric gaps.
Their spin-down timescale is $\tau = P/2\dot{P}$, and their gamma-ray emission fades over millions of years as the pulsar decelerates.
Over longer timescales, neutron stars in binary systems can undergo a recycling process: the older neutron star accretes matter and angular momentum from its companion, spinning back up to periods of 2--10 milliseconds \cite{1982Natur.300..728A}.
These millisecond pulsars (MSPs) possess weaker magnetic fields ($10^8$--$10^9$~G) but maintain their rapid rotation for gigayears.
While young pulsars remain tightly clustered near their birthplaces in the Galactic disk, MSPs have orbited the Galaxy numerous times.
Their natal kicks and prolonged dynamical history broaden their spatial distribution, causing MSP populations to extend to higher Galactic latitudes and to densely populate the Galactic bulge \cite{Calore:2014oga}.

The gamma-ray spectrum of MSPs poses a direct challenge for dark matter indirect detection.
Curvature radiation produces a characteristic power-law spectrum with an exponential cutoff at a few GeV.
This spectral shape is essentially indistinguishable from the expected photon yield of a $\sim$50~GeV WIMP annihilating into $b\bar{b}$ quarks \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.
Because the spectral shapes overlap so precisely, an unresolved population of thousands of MSPs in the Galactic bulge provides a compelling astrophysical explanation for the Galactic Center Excess (GCE).

Whether MSPs can fully account for the GCE depends on their gamma-ray luminosity function, the distribution of individual source luminosities within the population.
Field MSPs detected in the Galactic plane have mean luminosities of $\langle L_\gamma \rangle \sim 10^{33}$--$10^{34}$~erg/s \cite{Hooper:2015jlu}, but the bulge population probed by the GCE may differ in age, metallicity, and binary history.
Breaking the spectral degeneracy between WIMP annihilation and unresolved MSPs therefore requires independent constraints on this luminosity function.
One effective approach is to measure it in relatively clean environments with known distances, such as globular clusters.
These dense stellar environments act as efficient factories for the binary systems necessary for MSP recycling.
By characterizing the luminosity function of globular cluster MSPs, one can derive constraints that can be extrapolated to the broader Galactic bulge population.
This is the approach adopted in Chapter~4, where we use simulation-based inference to measure the MSP luminosity function and scrutinize the origin of the GCE.

Among the remaining Galactic gamma-ray sources, supernova remnants and pulsar wind nebulae are routinely detected by the Fermi-LAT but represent subdominant backgrounds for the dark matter searches undertaken in this thesis.
Having surveyed the Galactic foreground and its point-source populations, we now turn to the extragalactic sky.

\subsection{Extragalactic Sources}
\label{sec:extragalactic_sources}

The high-latitude sky is dominated by the Extragalactic Gamma-Ray Background (EGB), which encompasses the total gamma-ray emission originating outside our Galaxy.
The EGB combines both individually resolved extragalactic sources and the unresolved glow of fainter objects \cite{DGRB-review, Ajello:2015mfa}.
Within this total budget, the Isotropic Diffuse Gamma-Ray Background (IGRB) is the residual, statistically isotropic emission that remains after subtracting the Galactic Diffuse Emission and all cataloged point sources \cite{Ackermann:2014usa, DGRB-review}.
The Fermi-LAT has measured the IGRB from 100~MeV to 820~GeV, and characterizing its composition is essential for identifying the origins of high-energy cosmic radiation and for constraining dark matter annihilation.

The dominant contributors to the extragalactic sky are blazars, a subclass of active galactic nuclei (AGNs) with relativistic jets oriented along the observer's line of sight.
Accretion onto a supermassive black hole powers these jets, producing a bimodal Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) with a low-frequency synchrotron peak and a high-frequency inverse-Compton peak \cite{DGRB-review}.
Blazars are classified into two populations: Flat-Spectrum Radio Quasars (FSRQs) and BL Lacertae objects (BL Lacs).
FSRQs exhibit broad optical emission lines and typically have lower-frequency synchrotron peaks, resulting in softer gamma-ray spectra that contribute most to the background below 1~GeV.
BL Lacs lack strong emission lines, have harder energy spectra, and frequently exhibit high-frequency synchrotron peaks.
As a result, nearby, low-luminosity BL Lacs produce the majority of the EGB emission above 100~GeV \cite{Ajello:2015mfa}.
At these energies, gamma rays interact with the Extragalactic Background Light (EBL) to produce electron-positron pairs, an attenuation effect that naturally explains the exponential cutoff observed in the EGB spectrum above roughly 250~GeV.
As a unified class, resolved and unresolved blazars account for approximately $50^{+12}_{-11}$\% of the total EGB photons \cite{Ajello:2015mfa}.
Because they define both the resolved catalogs and the upper end of the unresolved background, the classification and modeling of blazars are central to the research presented in Sections~\ref{ch:dnds}, \ref{ch:dnds_catalog}, and~\ref{ch:dm_halos}.

The remainder of the extragalactic gamma-ray budget is primarily supplied by misaligned AGNs and star-forming galaxies (SFGs).
Misaligned AGNs, including Fanaroff-Riley radio galaxies, possess jets oriented away from our line of sight \cite{DGRB-review}.
Without strong Doppler boosting, they are intrinsically fainter than blazars but far more abundant, contributing an estimated 20\% to 25\% of the IGRB \cite{DGRB-review, DiMauro:2013xta}.
Star-forming galaxies, on the other hand, produce gamma rays through hadronic interactions as described in Section~\ref{sec:hadronic}: cosmic-ray protons accelerated by supernovae collide with interstellar gas to produce neutral pions that decay into gamma rays.
While their individual luminosities are low, SFGs are ubiquitous and their aggregate emission closely tracks the cosmic star-formation history.
Current models suggest they account for 10\% to 30\% of the EGB \cite{Ajello:2015mfa, Tamborra:2014xia}, potentially dominating among the faintest source populations.
Detailed population models demonstrate that the combined emission from blazars, misaligned AGNs, and SFGs can account for the entire amplitude and spectral shape of the measured EGB, establishing tight constraints on any dark matter contribution \cite{Ajello:2015mfa}.

The connection between resolved source catalogs and the unresolved background is formalized by the source-count distribution, $dN/dS$, which describes the differential number density of sources per unit solid angle as a function of their gamma-ray flux $S$.
Integrating the $dN/dS$ distribution from zero flux up to the telescope's point-source detection threshold yields the intensity of the unresolved IGRB.
While the bright end of the $dN/dS$ is populated by directly observable sources, probing the distribution below the detection threshold requires statistical techniques that go beyond individual source detection.
One effective approach uses photon-count probability distribution functions (1-point PDF or $P(D)$ statistics), which analyze the statistical fluctuations of pixel intensities across the gamma-ray sky map \cite{malyshev2011statistics, DGRB-review}.

Different source populations imprint distinct statistical signatures on the pixel counts.
A truly diffuse background, or a population of exceedingly faint and uniformly distributed sources such as SFGs, yields a Poissonian distribution of photon counts.
A population of discrete, relatively brighter unresolved point sources such as blazars, on the other hand, occasionally deposits multiple photons into single pixels, generating a non-Poissonian distribution with a heavy tail \cite{malyshev2011statistics}.
By modeling these overlapping statistical fingerprints with probability generating functions, the break and slopes of the $dN/dS$ distribution can be reconstructed deep into the unresolved regime.
Characterizing this $dN/dS$ distribution is the central observational objective of Chapter~\ref{ch:6}, where we use simulation-based inference to extract the properties of unresolved source populations from the high-latitude sky.
