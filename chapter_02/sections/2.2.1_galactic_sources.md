### 2.2.1 Galactic Sources

The gamma-ray emission from the Milky Way comprises two broad components: a diffuse foreground and a diverse population of individual point sources. The Galactic Diffuse Emission (GDE) dominates the observed sky, accounting for approximately 80\% of all photons detected by the Fermi-LAT \cite{DGRB-review}. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:production_mechanisms}, this emission arises from cosmic-ray interactions with the interstellar medium and radiation fields: hadronic pion production traces the distribution of interstellar gas along the Galactic plane, while inverse-Compton scattering off starlight and the CMB produces a more extended high-latitude component. To isolate other astrophysical signals, the standard approach relies on template fitting, in which the spatial and spectral distribution of the GDE is modeled using cosmic-ray propagation codes such as GALPROP \cite{Ackermann:2014usa, DGRB-review}. The Fermi-LAT sky model decomposes the observed counts into a linear combination of these GDE templates, individually resolved point sources, and a residual isotropic component. However, this decomposition depends on the assumed interstellar emission model (IEM), and the use of different IEM templates leads to systematic differences in the extracted residuals. Unmodeled gas structures, uncertainties in the cosmic-ray source distribution, and poorly constrained radiation fields all contribute to making the GDE template the largest source of systematic uncertainty in Fermi-LAT analyses. This mismodeling directly induces dataset shift when applying simulation-trained algorithms to real data, a challenge addressed in Chapter~5.

Beyond the diffuse foreground, the Galactic plane is populated by individual energetic sources, with pulsars being the most prominent gamma-ray emitters. Young pulsars are rapidly spinning neutron stars with large surface magnetic fields ($10^{11}$--$10^{13}$~G), emitting gamma rays primarily through curvature radiation in their magnetospheric gaps. Their spin-down timescale is $\tau = P/2\dot{P}$, and their gamma-ray emission fades over millions of years as the pulsar decelerates. Over longer timescales, neutron stars in binary systems can undergo a recycling process: the older neutron star accretes matter and angular momentum from its companion, spinning back up to periods of 2--10 milliseconds \cite{1982Natur.300..728A}. These millisecond pulsars (MSPs) possess weaker magnetic fields ($10^8$--$10^9$~G) but maintain their rapid rotation for gigayears. While young pulsars remain tightly clustered near their birthplaces in the Galactic disk, MSPs have orbited the Galaxy numerous times. Their natal kicks and prolonged dynamical history broaden their spatial distribution, causing MSP populations to extend to higher Galactic latitudes and to densely populate the Galactic bulge \cite{Calore:2014oga}.

The gamma-ray spectrum of MSPs poses a direct challenge for dark matter indirect detection. Curvature radiation produces a characteristic power-law spectrum with an exponential cutoff at a few GeV. This spectral shape is essentially indistinguishable from the expected photon yield of a $\sim$50~GeV WIMP annihilating into $b\bar{b}$ quarks \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Because the spectral shapes overlap so precisely, an unresolved population of thousands of MSPs in the Galactic bulge provides a compelling astrophysical explanation for the Galactic Center Excess (GCE).

Whether MSPs can fully account for the GCE depends on their gamma-ray luminosity function, the distribution of individual source luminosities within the population. Field MSPs detected in the Galactic plane have mean luminosities of $\langle L_\gamma \rangle \sim 10^{33}$--$10^{34}$~erg/s \cite{Hooper:2015jlu}, but the bulge population probed by the GCE may differ in age, metallicity, and binary history. Breaking the spectral degeneracy between WIMP annihilation and unresolved MSPs therefore requires independent constraints on this luminosity function. One effective approach is to measure it in relatively clean environments with known distances, such as globular clusters. These dense stellar environments act as efficient factories for the binary systems necessary for MSP recycling. By characterizing the luminosity function of globular cluster MSPs, one can derive constraints that can be extrapolated to the broader Galactic bulge population. This is the approach adopted in Chapter~4, where we use simulation-based inference to measure the MSP luminosity function and scrutinize the origin of the GCE.

Among the remaining Galactic gamma-ray sources, supernova remnants and pulsar wind nebulae are routinely detected by the Fermi-LAT but represent subdominant backgrounds for the dark matter searches undertaken in this thesis. Having surveyed the Galactic foreground and its point-source populations, we now turn to the extragalactic sky.
