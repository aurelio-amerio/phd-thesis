## 6.2.2 The 1-Point Photon-Count Distribution
\label{sec:6.2.2}

Recovering the source-count distribution below the detection threshold requires extracting population information from the collective statistical imprint of unresolved sources on the photon-count map. The photon-count distribution — also called the 1-point probability distribution function (1pPDF) — provides a method for doing so that has been developed and refined since its introduction in 2011.

The basic idea is simple. Given a pixelated sky map, one constructs the histogram of the number of pixels containing exactly $k$ photons. Different populations of gamma-ray emitters leave different imprints on this histogram \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}. Bright but rare sources, such as blazars just below the detection limit, produce multiple photons per source during the observation period. Because these photons are spatially correlated — they originate from the same point — they populate pixels with moderately large photon counts, generating non-Poissonian tails in the distribution. By contrast, faint but numerous sources, such as star-forming galaxies, contribute on average far less than one photon each; their combined signal is statistically indistinguishable from a smooth Poissonian background \cite{Malyshev:2011zi}. Analyzing the shape of the photon-count distribution — and in particular the departures from Poisson statistics — thus encodes information about the sub-threshold source population and its $dN/dS$.

The mathematical framework that makes this precise is the generating function formalism \cite{Malyshev:2011zi}. For a discrete probability distribution $p_k$ (the probability of finding $k$ photons in a given pixel), the generating function is defined as the power series $P(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} p_k t^k$. The central property of this formalism is that independent emission components multiply: if the observed photon count is the sum of contributions from point sources, Galactic foreground, and isotropic background, then the total generating function is the product of the individual generating functions of each component \cite{Malyshev:2011zi, Fornasa:2015qua}. For a population of point sources, the generating function takes the form
\begin{equation}
P_\mathrm{PS}(t) = \exp\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(x_m\, t^m - x_m)\right),
\label{eq:1ppdf_gf}
\end{equation}
where $x_m$ is the average number of sources per pixel emitting exactly $m$ photons during the observation time. The set $\{x_m\}$ can be derived from the $dN/dS$, and the parameters of the source-count distribution are then obtained by fitting the predicted photon-count probability to the observed pixel histogram via maximum likelihood.

Malyshev and Hogg \cite{Malyshev:2011zi} pioneered this approach for gamma rays, applying it to 11 months of Fermi-LAT data. They modeled the photon-count probabilities as the product of three generating functions — point sources, Galactic diffuse emission, and isotropic background — and constrained the contribution of unresolved AGN-like sources to the total flux, finding it to be approximately $17 \pm 2\%$ at high Galactic latitudes for photon energies above 1 GeV. Zechlin, Cuoco et al. \cite{Zechlin:2015wdz} improved the method significantly by adopting a pixel-dependent approach: rather than averaging the generating function across the sky, they incorporated the spatial variation of the Galactic foreground and exposure into the pixel-level probability calculation. Using 6 years of Fermi-LAT data, this pixel-dependent 1pPDF measured the $dN/dS$ in the 1--10 GeV band down to fluxes approximately one order of magnitude below the Fermi-LAT detection threshold. Subsequent work by the same group \cite{Zechlin:2016pme} and by Lisanti et al. \cite{Lisanti:2016jub} extended the technique to multiple energy bands spanning 1--171 GeV, providing the first energy-dependent measurements of the unresolved source-count distribution.

It is worth noting that the same pixel-statistics formalism was later adapted for a different purpose: the Non-Poissonian Template Fit (NPTF) applied to the Galactic Center Excess (see Section~\ref{sec:4.4.1}). In that context, the method was used to distinguish between a smooth dark matter signal and a population of unresolved point sources (such as millisecond pulsars) near the Galactic Center. The versatility of photon-count methods across these very different science cases — from extragalactic population statistics to the GCE debate — illustrates their broad applicability. Having established how the 1pPDF extracts $dN/dS$ from the pixel statistics, we now turn to why the source-count distribution itself is a powerful diagnostic of the unresolved gamma-ray sky.
