### 6.3.2 The SBI Approach: Learning from Simulated Maps
\label{sec:6.3.2}

The limitations discussed above share a common root: the analytical 1pPDF requires an explicit, tractable likelihood whose evaluation imposes the simplifications just described.  A natural alternative is to bypass the likelihood entirely and instead learn the mapping from data to parameters through simulated examples.  This is the simulation-based inference (SBI) strategy introduced in Chapter~\ref{ch:3} (\S\ref{sec:3.2}), applied here to the recovery of the sub-threshold source-count distribution.

The starting point is the observation that the forward model — from a set of $dN/dS$ parameters to a realistic photon-count map — is straightforward to simulate, even though the corresponding likelihood is computationally expensive to evaluate.  Source fluxes are drawn from the $dN/dS$, placed at random positions on the sky, convolved with the Fermi-LAT point spread function, and added to a model of the Galactic foreground and a constant isotropic background.  The resulting flux map is then multiplied by the exposure map and subjected to Poisson noise, producing a synthetic photon-count map that reproduces the statistical properties of real Fermi-LAT observations \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.  By generating a large ensemble of such maps — each corresponding to a different draw of the $dN/dS$ parameters — one obtains a training set that implicitly encodes the full complexity of the forward model, including PSF-induced inter-pixel correlations and exposure variations that the analytical formalism treats only approximately.

A convolutional neural network (CNN) is then trained on this ensemble to learn the inverse mapping: given a foreground-subtracted photon-count map as input, the network outputs the $dN/dS$ evaluated in 20 logarithmically spaced flux bins covering the range $5 \times 10^{-12}$ to $10^{-7}$~cm$^{-2}$~s$^{-1}$, together with the isotropic background flux $F_\mathrm{iso}$ \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.  This non-parametric discretisation allows the network to reconstruct arbitrary $dN/dS$ shapes without imposing a broken power-law prior, in contrast to the analytical approach.  In total, $9 \times 10^5$ synthetic maps are used for training and $10^5$ for validation.

The network's error estimation combines two complementary strategies.  The first is a heteroscedastic Gaussian loss function, in which the network predicts both the mean $\hat{y}_i$ and the variance $\hat{\sigma}_i^2$ for each output bin \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.  This cost function contains two competing terms: one penalises large residuals weighted by $1/\hat{\sigma}_i^2$, the other penalises inflated variances.  The balance between them produces bin-wise uncertainties that automatically widen at low and high fluxes, where the network reaches its confusion limit, without requiring any manual tuning.  The second strategy is concrete dropout \cite{Gal:2017concrete}, a trainable form of dropout placed before each convolutional layer, which allows the network to learn its own regularisation strength during training.  Together, these components approximate a Bayesian posterior over the outputs.  As an independent cross-check, a frequentist error estimation is constructed from the distribution of residuals across the $10^5$ validation maps, confirming that the Bayesian uncertainties are well calibrated \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.

This framework addresses the four limitations identified in \S\ref{sec:6.3.1}: PSF correlations are implicitly captured through the spatial structure of the training maps, the $dN/dS$ is recovered non-parametrically in flux bins rather than as a parametric power law, and the architecture can incorporate multiple energy bands as additional input channels (\S\ref{sec:6.3.1}).  Above all, the inference is amortised: while training on $9 \times 10^5$ maps requires substantial computational investment, applying the trained network to new data amounts to a single forward pass.  One practical challenge remains: applying a standard CNN to data defined on the sphere without introducing geometric distortions, which we address next.
