## 6.2.3 Why dN/dS Is a Powerful Observable
\label{sec:6.2.3}

The source-count distribution is more than a statistical summary of the faint source population — it is a direct diagnostic of the composition and evolution of the gamma-ray sky. Its power lies in the fact that different astrophysical source classes contribute to different portions of the flux range, so that the shape of $dN/dS$ encodes information about the relative abundance and properties of each population.

At the bright end, blazars — both BL Lac objects and flat-spectrum radio quasars — dominate the resolved catalog and the high-flux source counts \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}. At progressively fainter fluxes, other populations become relevant: misaligned AGNs, star-forming galaxies, and potentially more exotic contributors occupy the sub-threshold regime where direct detection fails. Decomposing the $dN/dS$ into these individual components constrains the luminosity functions of populations that are otherwise accessible only through indirect multi-wavelength correlations. The 1pPDF-based studies by Korsmeier et al. \cite{Korsmeier:2022cwp}, Manconi et al. \cite{Manconi:2019ynl}, and Di Mauro et al. \cite{DiMauro:2017ing} demonstrated that photon-count statistics can isolate the blazar contribution to the unresolved sky and constrain its energy-dependent $dN/dS$, thereby probing the faint end of the blazar luminosity function.

Integrating the sub-threshold $dN/dS$ yields the total contribution of unresolved point sources to the UGRB — a quantity that carries direct implications for the composition of the diffuse gamma-ray sky. Current estimates indicate that the guaranteed astrophysical populations (blazars, misaligned AGNs, star-forming galaxies, and millisecond pulsars) can collectively account for much of the observed background intensity \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}. Blazars contribute approximately 20\% of the UGRB between 0.1 and 100 GeV, though high-synchrotron-peaked BL Lacs dominate the background above a few tens of GeV. Misaligned AGNs account for an estimated $\sim 25\%$ above 100 MeV, while star-forming galaxies contribute between a few percent and $\sim 50\%$ below 10 GeV, depending on the assumed evolutionary model. Millisecond pulsars are subdominant, contributing less than 1\% of the UGRB intensity. The tighter the observational constraints on $dN/dS$, the smaller the residual room for non-standard contributions — including dark matter annihilation — to the background.

This diagnostic power extends in two directions that are directly relevant to this thesis. First, the $dN/dS$ measured in this chapter provides the empirical prior for the probabilistic cataloging framework developed in Chapter 7, where it is used to determine the expected flux distribution of sources just below the detection threshold. Second, energy-dependent decomposition of the $dN/dS$ into individual source classes opens the possibility of searching for spectral signatures of a dark matter component — a natural extension of the methodology that remains a goal for future work. The complementary approach of searching for collective dark matter emission through angular cross-correlations with tracers of large-scale structure is addressed in Chapter 8.

The analytical 1pPDF approach reviewed in the preceding subsection has been remarkably successful, but it operates under assumptions that limit its scope. The generating function framework requires a parametric model for the $dN/dS$ (typically a multiply broken power law), and the inference is not amortized: each new dataset or parameter configuration requires a full re-convergence of the likelihood, which is computationally expensive. The next section introduces a simulation-based alternative that relaxes these assumptions and enables non-parametric, amortized inference of the source-count distribution.
