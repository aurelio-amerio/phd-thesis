\section{The Source-Count Distribution}
\label{sec:source_count}

The analysis of the unresolved gamma-ray sky begins with a single observable: the differential source-count distribution, $dN/dS$.
This function describes how many point sources exist at each flux level and provides the most direct statistical characterization of the populations that contribute to the unresolved gamma-ray background.
Its reconstruction below the telescope detection threshold --- where individual sources can no longer be identified --- is the central inference problem addressed in this chapter.

This section introduces the $dN/dS$ and the methodological context necessary to understand the approach developed in Section~\ref{sec:sbi_cnn}. We begin with the formal definition and its connection to the underlying luminosity function (Section~\ref{sec:6.2.1}), then review the 1-point photon-count distribution, the principal analytical method used to extract $dN/dS$ from sub-threshold data (Section~\ref{sec:6.2.2}). We close by discussing why the source-count distribution is such a valuable diagnostic of the gamma-ray sky and how it connects to the broader goals of this thesis (Section~\ref{sec:6.2.3}).

\subsection{Definition and Physical Meaning}
\label{sec:6.2.1}

The differential source-count distribution, $dN/dS$, gives the number of sources per unit solid angle per unit flux at a given integral photon flux $S$ in a specified energy band.
It is the fundamental observable that describes how point sources are distributed as a function of their apparent brightness, and it connects directly to the physics of the emitting populations.

For sources bright enough to be individually cataloged, $dN/dS$ is measured by simply counting objects in the catalog as a function of their flux (see also Section~\ref{sec:astro_sky}). The 4FGL-DR3 catalog \cite{2022ApJS..260...53A}, based on 12 years of Fermi-LAT data, contains 6658 resolved sources and provides a direct measurement of the source-count distribution in the resolved regime \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.
Above the flux threshold where the catalog detection efficiency reaches 100\%, this measurement is straightforward.
Below this threshold, the detection efficiency degrades, and direct counting must be supplemented by Monte Carlo corrections to reconstruct the true underlying distribution.
Further below --- in the unresolved regime, where individual sources are too faint to be detected at all --- the cumulative emission of these sources forms the unresolved gamma-ray background (UGRB), and the $dN/dS$ must be inferred from the statistical properties of the photon-count map.
This reconstruction is the central challenge addressed by this chapter.

The source-count distribution is the observational projection of a more fundamental quantity: the luminosity function.
The gamma-ray luminosity function, $\Phi_\gamma(z, L_\gamma)$, gives the comoving number density of sources per unit luminosity $L_\gamma$ as a function of redshift $z$.
The connection between the two is given by \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}

\begin{equation}
	\frac{dN}{dS} = \int_{z_\mathrm{min}}^{z_\mathrm{max}} dz \int_{\Gamma_\mathrm{min}}^{\Gamma_\mathrm{max}} d\Gamma \; \frac{dN}{dV \, dL_\gamma \, d\Gamma}(z, L_\gamma, \Gamma) \; \frac{dV}{dz \, d\Omega} \; \frac{dL_\gamma}{dS},
	\label{eq:dnds_lf}
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is a parameter that characterizes the energy spectrum shape of the source, $dV/(dz \, d\Omega)$ is the comoving volume element per unit redshift and solid angle, and $dL_\gamma/dS$ is the Jacobian relating luminosity to observed flux through the luminosity distance $D_L(z)$.
The integrand $dN/(dV \, dL_\gamma \, d\Gamma)$ --- the comoving density of sources per unit luminosity and spectral index --- is the luminosity function itself, which encodes the intrinsic abundance of sources at each cosmic epoch.

This integral makes precise the physical content of $dN/dS$: it takes the intrinsic distribution of sources in luminosity and redshift, projects it through the flux--distance relation, and integrates over all redshifts and spectral shapes to produce the observed flux distribution.
In other words, $dN/dS$ can be interpreted as the integral along the line of sight of the gamma-ray luminosity function. It is what we observe when we look at the sky, while the luminosity function is the astrophysical quantity we ultimately want to constrain.

The source-count distribution therefore bridges two distinct observational regimes.
At the bright end, it is anchored by direct measurements from the Fermi-LAT catalog, which shows a flux distribution broadly compatible with an $S^{-2}$ power law (shallower than the Euclidean expectation $S^{-5/2}$) across most of the resolved range \cite{Amerio:2023uet}.
At the faint end, below the detection threshold of approximately $S_\mathrm{th} \sim 2 \times 10^{-10}$~cm$^{-2}$~s$^{-1}$ in the 1--10 GeV band, the $dN/dS$ must be reconstructed statistically from the collective imprint of unresolved sources on the photon-count map.
The techniques for performing this reconstruction --- and for extending the $dN/dS$ well below the Fermi-LAT threshold --- are the subject of the following subsections.

\subsection{The 1-Point Photon-Count Distribution}
\label{sec:6.2.2}
\aure{maybe shrink this section a bit?}

Recovering the source-count distribution below the detection threshold requires extracting population information from the collective statistical imprint of unresolved sources on the photon-count map.
The photon-count distribution --- also called the 1-point probability distribution function (1pPDF) --- provides a method for doing so that has been developed and refined since its introduction in 2011.

Given a pixelated sky map, one constructs the histogram of the number of pixels containing exactly $k$ photons.
Different populations of gamma-ray emitters have different effects on this histogram \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}.
Bright but rare sources, such as blazars just below the detection limit, produce multiple photons per source during the observation period.
Because these photons are spatially correlated --- they originate from the same point --- they populate pixels with moderately large photon counts, generating non-Poissonian tails in the distribution.
By contrast, faint but numerous sources, such as star-forming galaxies, contribute on average far less than one photon each; their combined signal is statistically indistinguishable from a smooth Poissonian background \cite{Malyshev:2011zi}.
Analyzing the shape of the photon-count distribution --- and in particular the departures from Poisson statistics --- thus encodes information about the sub-threshold source population and its $dN/dS$.

The mathematical framework that makes this precise is the generating function formalism \cite{Malyshev:2011zi}.
For a discrete probability distribution $p_k$ (the probability of finding $k$ photons in a given pixel), the generating function is defined as the power series $P(t) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} p_k t^k$.
The central property of this formalism is that independent emission components multiply: if the observed photon count is the sum of contributions from point sources, Galactic foreground, and isotropic background, then the total generating function is the product of the individual generating functions of each component \cite{Malyshev:2011zi, Fornasa:2015qua}. For a population of point sources, the generating function takes the form
\begin{equation}
	P_\mathrm{PS}(t) = \exp\!
	\left(\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}(x_m\, t^m - x_m)\right),
	\label{eq:1ppdf_gf}
\end{equation}
where $x_m$ is the average number of sources per pixel emitting exactly $m$ photons during the observation time.
The set $\{x_m\}$ can be derived from the $dN/dS$, and the parameters of the source-count distribution are then obtained by fitting the predicted photon-count probability to the observed pixel histogram via maximum likelihood.

Malyshev and Hogg \cite{Malyshev:2011zi} pioneered this approach for gamma rays, applying it to 11 months of Fermi-LAT data.
They modeled the photon-count probabilities as the product of three generating functions --- point sources, Galactic diffuse emission, and isotropic background --- and constrained the contribution of unresolved AGN-like sources to the total flux, finding it to be approximately $17 \pm 2\%$ at high Galactic latitudes for photon energies above 1 GeV.
Zechlin, Cuoco et al.
\ \cite{Zechlin:2015wdz} improved the method significantly by adopting a pixel-dependent approach: rather than averaging the generating function across the sky, they incorporated the spatial variation of the Galactic foreground and exposure into the pixel-level probability calculation.
Using 6 years of Fermi-LAT data, this pixel-dependent 1pPDF measured the $dN/dS$ in the 1--10 GeV band down to fluxes approximately one order of magnitude below the Fermi-LAT detection threshold.
Subsequent work by the same group \cite{Zechlin:2016pme} and by Lisanti et al.\ \cite{Lisanti:2016jub} extended the technique to multiple energy bands spanning 1--171 GeV, providing the first energy-dependent measurements of the unresolved source-count distribution, albeit in independent energy bins.

It is worth noting that the same pixel-statistics formalism was later adapted for a different purpose: the Non-Poissonian Template Fit (NPTF) applied to the Galactic Center Excess (see Section~\ref{sec:4.4}).
In that context, the method was used to distinguish between a smooth dark matter signal and a population of unresolved point sources (such as millisecond pulsars) near the Galactic Center.
The versatility of photon-count methods across these very different science cases --- from extragalactic population statistics to the GCE debate --- illustrates their broad applicability.
Having established how the 1pPDF extracts $dN/dS$ from the pixel statistics, we now turn to why the source-count distribution itself is a powerful diagnostic of the unresolved gamma-ray sky.

\subsection{Why $dN/dS$ Is a Powerful Observable}
\label{sec:6.2.3}

The source-count distribution is more than a statistical summary of the faint source population --- it is a direct diagnostic of the composition and evolution of the gamma-ray sky.
Its power lies in the fact that different astrophysical source classes contribute to different portions of the flux range, so that the shape of $dN/dS$ encodes information about the relative abundance and properties of each population.

At the bright end, blazars --- both BL Lac objects and flat-spectrum radio quasars --- dominate the resolved catalog and the high-flux source counts \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}.
At progressively fainter fluxes, other populations become relevant: misaligned AGNs, star-forming galaxies, and potentially more exotic contributors occupy the sub-threshold regime where direct detection fails.
Decomposing the $dN/dS$ into these individual components constrains the luminosity functions of populations that are otherwise accessible only through indirect multi-wavelength correlations.
The 1pPDF-based studies by \red{Korsmeier et al.
\\cite{Korsmeier:2022cwp}, Manconi et al.\\cite{Manconi:2019ynl}, and Di Mauro et al.\\cite{DiMauro:2017ing}} demonstrated that photon-count statistics can isolate the blazar contribution to the unresolved sky and constrain its energy-dependent $dN/dS$, thereby probing the faint end of the blazar luminosity function.

Integrating the sub-threshold $dN/dS$ yields the total contribution of unresolved point sources to the UGRB --- a quantity that carries direct implications for the composition of the diffuse gamma-ray sky.
Current estimates indicate that the guaranteed astrophysical populations (blazars, misaligned AGNs, star-forming galaxies, and millisecond pulsars) can collectively account for much of the observed background intensity \cite{Fornasa:2015qua}.
Blazars contribute approximately 20\% of the UGRB between 0.1 and 100 GeV, though high-synchrotron-peaked BL Lacs dominate the background above a few tens of GeV.
Misaligned AGNs account for an estimated ${\sim}\,25\%$ above 100 MeV, while star-forming galaxies contribute between a few percent and ${\sim}\,50\%$ below 10 GeV, depending on the assumed evolutionary model.
Millisecond pulsars are subdominant, contributing less than 1\% of the UGRB intensity.
The tighter the observational constraints on $dN/dS$, the smaller the residual room for non-standard contributions --- including dark matter annihilation --- to the background.

This diagnostic power extends in two directions that are directly relevant to this thesis.
First, the $dN/dS$ measured in this chapter provides the empirical prior for the probabilistic cataloging framework developed in Chapter~\ref{ch:7}, where it is used to determine the expected flux distribution of sources just below the detection threshold.
Second, energy-dependent decomposition of the $dN/dS$ into individual source classes opens the possibility of searching for spectral signatures of a dark matter component --- a natural extension of the methodology that remains a goal for future work.
The complementary approach of searching for collective dark matter emission through angular cross-correlations with tracers of large-scale structure is addressed in Chapter~\ref{ch:8}.

The analytical 1pPDF approach reviewed in the preceding subsection has been remarkably successful, but it operates under assumptions that limit its scope.
The generating function framework requires a parametric model for the $dN/dS$ (typically a multiply broken power law), and the inference is not amortized: each new dataset or parameter configuration requires a full re-convergence of the likelihood, which is computationally expensive.
The next section introduces a simulation-based alternative that relaxes these assumptions and enables non-parametric, amortized inference of the source-count distribution.
