# 5.2.2 Luminous Satellites vs. Dark Subhalos

\label{sec:luminous_vs_dark}

Not all subhalos are alike from an observational standpoint. Above a mass threshold of approximately $10^8\,M_\odot$, a subhalo possesses sufficient gravitational potential to accrete and retain baryonic matter, enabling star formation and producing a visible dwarf galaxy \cite{Zavala:2019gpd}. Roughly 50 such satellite galaxies are currently known within the virial radius of the Milky Way \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}, ranging from the classical dwarf spheroidals --- Draco, Sculptor, Fornax --- to the ultra-faint systems discovered over the past two decades by digital sky surveys such as the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Dark Energy Survey \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. These dwarf spheroidal galaxies are among the most dark-matter-dominated objects known, with mass-to-light ratios exceeding $\sim 1000$ in some cases, and they have been extensively used as targets for indirect dark matter searches in gamma rays (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:indirect_detection_ch1}).

Below $\sim 10^8\,M_\odot$, however, galaxy formation becomes progressively inefficient. The ultraviolet background produced by cosmic reionization suppresses gas accretion in halos with virial masses below $\sim 10^9\,M_\odot$, while atomic cooling ceases to operate below $\sim 10^8\,M_\odot$ \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. The vast majority of dark matter subhalos therefore contain no stars, no gas, and produce no conventional electromagnetic signature. These are the truly ``dark'' subhalos: structures whose existence is predicted with high confidence by $\Lambda$CDM but which cannot be detected through traditional astronomical observations. If dark matter annihilates, however, these objects could shine in gamma rays, and it is precisely this population that we target in the analysis presented in this chapter.

The apparent discrepancy between the predicted abundance of subhalos and the observed number of satellite galaxies was historically known as the ``missing satellite problem'' \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. This tension has been largely resolved on two fronts. Observationally, improved surveys have steadily increased the census of ultra-faint dwarf galaxies, and statistical corrections for sky coverage and detection efficiency suggest that the total satellite population is larger than the pre-2004 sample indicated \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. Theoretically, hydrodynamical simulations including baryonic physics --- UV feedback, supernova-driven outflows, reionization --- naturally suppress galaxy formation in low-mass halos, bringing predictions into broad agreement with observations once detection efficiencies are accounted for \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}.

Baryonic processes also modify the survival of the subhalos themselves. Tidal stripping by the host halo's gravitational field removes dark matter from the outer layers of orbiting subhalos, progressively reducing their masses and sometimes leading to complete disruption \cite{2020MNRAS.491.4591E, 2018MNRAS.474.3043V}. Subhalos on orbits that cross the Galactic disk experience additional gravitational shocks --- disk shocking --- that can further loosen or destroy their structure \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. These environmental effects are most pronounced in the inner Galaxy, which is consequently more depleted of subhalos than the outskirts \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Hydrodynamical simulations such as the FIRE project \cite{2017MNRAS.471.1709G} and the Auriga suite \cite{2021MNRAS.501.3558G} have confirmed and quantified these effects for subhalos above their resolution limit of $\sim 10^6\,M_\odot$. Below this threshold, the survival rate of low-mass subhalos, particularly in the inner Milky Way, remains uncertain \cite{2023MNRAS.518...93A}, and this represents one of the principal sources of theoretical uncertainty in predicting the number of gamma-ray-detectable dark matter subhalos (cf.\ Section~\ref{sec:gamma_targets}).
