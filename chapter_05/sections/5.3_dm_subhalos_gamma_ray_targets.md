## Dark Matter Subhalos as Gamma-Ray Targets
\label{sec:5.3}

Having established that $\Lambda$CDM predicts a vast population of dark subhalos orbiting the Milky Way, we now turn to the question of whether any of these objects could be observed in gamma rays. The connection between the subhalo population of Section~\ref{sec:5.2} and actual observability depends on both the particle-physics properties of dark matter --- the mass $m_\mathrm{DM}$, the annihilation cross section $\langle\sigma v\rangle$, and the final-state channel --- and the astrophysical properties of each subhalo, encoded in its J-factor. We first review the expected gamma-ray signatures of individual subhalos, then discuss the range of detection predictions and the search strategy adopted in this work.

### Expected Gamma-Ray Properties
\label{sec:5.3.1}

The gamma-ray flux produced by dark matter annihilation in a subhalo is determined by the product of a particle-physics factor and an astrophysical factor. For a subhalo with J-factor $J$, the differential photon flux takes the form
$$
\Phi_\mathrm{DM}(E) = \frac{J}{4\pi}\,\frac{\langle\sigma v\rangle}{2\,m_\mathrm{DM}^2}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}N_\gamma}{\mathrm{d}E}\bigg|_\mathrm{ch.}\,,
$$
where $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ is the thermally averaged annihilation cross section, $m_\mathrm{DM}$ is the dark matter particle mass, and $\mathrm{d}N_\gamma/\mathrm{d}E$ is the gamma-ray energy spectrum per annihilation event in a given final-state channel \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. The J-factor encodes the line-of-sight integral of the squared dark matter density $\rho^2$ over the solid angle subtended by the source (see Section~\ref{sec:1.4.4} for the full derivation). Since $\Phi_\mathrm{DM}$ factorizes cleanly into a term that depends only on the particle model and one that depends only on the dark matter distribution, the two sources of uncertainty can be studied independently.

The spectral shape $\mathrm{d}N_\gamma/\mathrm{d}E$ depends on the annihilation channel. Hadronic channels such as $b\bar{b}$ produce soft spectra peaked well below the dark matter mass: the energetic quarks shower, fragment, and hadronize into a cascade of lighter mesons, and the subsequent decay $\pi^0 \to \gamma\gamma$ generates a broad bump at $E \sim m_\mathrm{DM}/20$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. Leptonic channels yield harder spectra that extend closer to $m_\mathrm{DM}$. The $\tau^+\tau^-$ channel, in particular, retains the characteristic hard profile of a leptonic final state while also producing a secondary hadronic component from the $\tau$ decay modes \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. In the analysis presented later in this chapter, the spectral energy distributions for each channel are computed with \textsc{CosmiXs} \cite{2024JCAP...03..035A}. For a fixed dark matter mass and annihilation channel, all subhalos share the same intrinsic spectrum; only the overall normalization differs, set by the J-factor of each individual object.

This spectral uniformity across the population is one of the defining features of a dark matter subhalo signal. Astrophysical source classes exhibit broad, heterogeneous spectral distributions driven by diverse physical conditions: extragalactic sources, dominated by AGNs, display a wide range of nearly power-law spectra, while Galactic sources such as pulsars present highly curved spectra spanning a similarly broad parameter range \cite{2022ApJS..260...53A}. A population of dark matter subhalos, by contrast, would occupy a tightly localized region in spectral parameter space, with the observed scatter arising purely from statistical measurement uncertainties rather than intrinsic physical differences \cite{Amerio:2025fhz}. This population-level coherence is what enables the statistical approach developed later in this chapter.

The brightest subhalos may also be marginally resolvable by Fermi-LAT. Coronado-Blázquez et al.\ \cite{2022PhRvD.105h3006C} showed that the most luminous Galactic subhalos, if detected, would appear as spatially extended sources with angular sizes of approximately $0.2^\circ$--$0.3^\circ$. Such an extension would provide a near-unambiguous identification, since astrophysical point sources rarely exhibit comparable spatial profiles at these angular scales. The vast majority of detectable subhalos, however, would appear point-like: their emission falls within the Fermi-LAT angular resolution of roughly $0.1^\circ$ in the GeV range \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Accordingly, most analyses compute J-factors integrated up to the $0.1^\circ$ angular radius rather than the full tidal radius of each subhalo, aligning the theoretical predictions with how these faint sources would actually appear in point-source catalogs \cite{Amerio:2025fhz, 2024MNRAS.530.2496A}.

The expected sky distribution of dark matter subhalos provides a further handle for separating them from astrophysical populations. Because subhalos trace the roughly spherical dark matter halo of the Milky Way, their projected distribution at high Galactic latitudes ($|b| > 10^\circ$) is approximately isotropic \cite{Amerio:2025fhz}. This contrasts with Galactic source populations such as pulsars and supernova remnants, which concentrate along the Galactic plane and toward the inner Galaxy \cite{2022ApJS..260...53A}. The isotropic distribution does, however, resemble that of extragalactic sources, primarily blazars, which dominate the high-latitude gamma-ray sky. Here the spectral properties introduced above become the primary discriminant: the combination of isotropic spatial distribution and spectral homogeneity provides the statistical foundation on which the population-level search presented in this chapter is built.

### Detection Predictions and Search Strategy
\label{sec:5.3.2}

The expected number of dark matter subhalos detectable by Fermi-LAT spans several orders of magnitude, depending on the assumed particle-physics and astrophysical parameters. Three factors dominate this uncertainty. First, the gamma-ray flux scales linearly with $\langle\sigma v\rangle$: larger annihilation cross sections push more subhalos above the Fermi-LAT detection threshold ($\mathrm{TS} > 25$), so the predicted count rises steeply with $\langle\sigma v\rangle$. Second, the J-factor prescription matters. J-factors can be computed from the enclosed mass ($M_\mathrm{sub}$) or from the maximum circular velocity ($V_\mathrm{max}$) of each subhalo; the latter is generally more reliable because it does not require assuming a specific density profile \cite{2024MNRAS.530.2496A}. A further choice arises from the integration radius: integrating the density-squared profile out to the full tidal radius ($J_\mathrm{tidal}$) captures all emission but implicitly assumes the subhalo is resolved, whereas integrating only to the $0.1^\circ$ angular radius ($J_{0.1}$) better represents how faint subhalos would appear as point sources in the catalog \cite{Amerio:2025fhz, 2022PhRvD.105h3006C}. Third, the survival of low-mass subhalos remains uncertain: the N-body simulations on which J-factor distributions are based lack baryonic physics, and hydrodynamical simulations do not yet resolve subhalos below $\sim 10^6\,M_\odot$ (Section~\ref{sec:5.2.2}). All three factors compound, so published predictions for the number of Fermi-LAT--detectable subhalos range from zero to several tens, depending on which combination of parameters is adopted \cite{2019JCAP...07..020C, 2019JCAP...11..045C, 2019Galax...7...90C, Amerio:2025fhz}.

This wide spread in predictions is not a weakness but a motivation: it defines the parameter space that a data-driven search must explore. The analysis proceeds in two stages. In the first stage, we ask whether the observed population of unassociated Fermi-LAT sources is better described by a model that includes a dark matter subhalo component or by one containing only known astrophysical source classes. The dark matter component is not arbitrary: its spectral distribution is fully determined by the assumed $m_\mathrm{DM}$ and annihilation channel via Monte Carlo simulations that draw J-factors from the N-body--derived distribution, compute the corresponding fluxes, and apply realistic detection efficiencies (see Section~\ref{sec:5.7}). In the second stage, if no statistically significant subhalo contribution is found, the profile likelihood is used to set 95\% confidence upper bounds on $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ as a function of $m_\mathrm{DM}$. Because the subhalo flux scales directly with $\langle\sigma v\rangle$, the absence of an excess translates directly into a constraint on the fundamental annihilation rate.

Both possible outcomes are scientifically informative. A positive detection would provide direct evidence that the $\Lambda$CDM-predicted subhalo population contains dark matter that annihilates into Standard Model particles. Dark subhalos are, in this respect, exceptionally clean targets: unlike the Galactic Center, where any potential dark matter signal is entangled with complex astrophysical foregrounds (Chapter~\ref{ch:4}), a subhalo below $\sim 10^8\,M_\odot$ contains no stars or gas and produces no conventional electromagnetic emission. Any gamma-ray signal from such an object would originate entirely from dark matter annihilation, making a detection particularly compelling. A null result, on the other hand, constrains $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ and provides an independent cross-check of the limits obtained from dwarf spheroidal galaxies \cite{2024PhRvD.109f3024M}, while also benchmarking the sensitivity of the current instrument and informing forecasts for next-generation telescopes such as CTA and SWGO. The quantitative J-factor distributions, detection threshold calculations, and the full Monte Carlo pipeline that implements this search strategy are presented in the paper body (Section~\ref{sec:5.7}).
