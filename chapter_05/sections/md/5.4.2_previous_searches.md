### Previous DM Subhalo Searches
\label{sec:5.4.2}

The search for dark matter subhalos among Fermi-LAT unassociated sources has a decade-long history, progressing through the successive data releases of the telescope. All prior efforts share a common strategy, referred to as *classify-and-count*: a set of DM subhalo candidates is selected from the unassociated population based on their gamma-ray properties, and upper limits on the annihilation cross section $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ are derived under the assumption that the number of true DM subhalos cannot exceed the number of identified candidates \cite{2010PhRvD..82f3501B, 2012A&A...538A..93Z, 2012ApJ...747..121A, 2019JCAP...07..020C}.

The earliest searches relied on hand-crafted selection criteria applied directly to catalog parameters. Buckley \& Hooper \cite{2010PhRvD..82f3501B} pioneered this approach by filtering unassociated sources in the 1FGL catalog according to spectral and spatial properties expected from annihilating WIMPs. Subsequent analyses by Zechlin et al. \cite{2012A&A...538A..93Z} and the Fermi-LAT Collaboration \cite{2012ApJ...747..121A} refined these criteria and extended the search to the 2FGL catalog. Berlin \& Hooper \cite{2014PhRvD..89a6014B} and Bertoni et al. \cite{2015JCAP...12..035B} placed particular emphasis on spatial extension as a potential discriminant, since nearby subhalos may subtend a measurable angular size whose radial profile would follow the DM density distribution rather than the telescope's point-spread function. Schoonenberg et al. \cite{2016JCAP...05..028S} and Hooper \& Witte \cite{2017JCAP...04..018H} applied updated theoretical predictions for DM gamma-ray emission to the 3FGL catalog. Calore et al. \cite{2017PhRvD..96f3009C} provided realistic detectability estimates, while Coronado-Blázquez et al. \cite{2019JCAP...07..020C} performed a comprehensive spectral and spatial analysis identifying 16 candidate sources, and in a companion paper \cite{2019JCAP...11..045C} derived the best achievable sensitivity of the 3FGL catalog under the assumption that zero unassociated sources are subhalos. A common thread across these analyses is that none of the identified candidates could be unambiguously distinguished from standard astrophysical sources: the gamma-ray spectrum of a DM subhalo can be equally well fit by a faint AGN or pulsar template, and the extension searches returned null results for all candidates.

More recently, machine learning classifiers have been applied to automate and extend the candidate selection. Mirabal et al. \cite{2016ApJ...825...69M} used supervised classification on the 3FGL catalog to evaluate pulsar and DM subhalo interpretations of the high-latitude unassociated population. Germán et al. \cite{2023MNRAS.520.1348G} constructed a generative model for Fermi-LAT sources incorporating systematic features, while Butter et al. \cite{2023JCAP...07..033B} applied Bayesian neural networks to the 4FGL catalog. In their most conservative classification scenario, Butter et al. identified 281 DM subhalo candidates. The resulting upper limits on $\langle\sigma v\rangle$ are correspondingly weak: the analysis assumes that the true number of subhalos cannot exceed this candidate count, and a different probability threshold would yield a different number and therefore different physical bounds.

A general weakness of the classify-and-count approach, whether carried out with hand-crafted cuts or ML classifiers, is that the final physical constraints depend on choices that lack a rigorous statistical foundation. Three problems are particularly severe. First, in ML-based analyses the number of DM candidates depends on an ad hoc probability threshold: setting $p_{\mathrm{DM}} > 0.5$ versus $p_{\mathrm{DM}} > 0.9$ can change the candidate count by an order of magnitude, with no statistical principle to prefer one threshold over another. Second, the classification probabilities themselves are calibrated on balanced training sets that use an artificial 50/50 split of astrophysical and DM-like sources. Since the true prevalence of DM subhalos is unknown and likely very small, this balanced training inflates the predicted DM probabilities relative to reality. Correcting this bias requires a realistic testing dataset of confirmed DM subhalos --- which does not exist. Third, all classify-and-count methods implicitly assume that the feature distribution of unassociated sources matches that of the associated training set, an assumption violated by the systematic differences documented in Section~\ref{sec:5.4.1} and analyzed formally in Section~\ref{sec:3.4}. These failures of classical classification motivate the shift toward quantification learning developed in the next section.
