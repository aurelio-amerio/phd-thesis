# 5.2.1 Hierarchical Structure Formation

\label{sec:hierarchical_formation}

A defining prediction of $\Lambda$CDM cosmology is that cosmic structure assembles in a bottom-up fashion. The primordial power spectrum, processed through the radiation-dominated epoch, retains more power on small spatial scales, so that smaller overdensities reach the nonlinear regime and gravitationally collapse before larger ones \cite{Frenk:2012AnP}. The resulting bound objects --- dark matter halos --- then grow through the continuous accretion of diffuse matter and the merging of pre-existing smaller halos, a process known as hierarchical structure formation \cite{Vogelsberger:2020NatRP}.

When a smaller halo is accreted by a larger host, it does not dissolve entirely into a smooth background. Because early-forming halos are highly concentrated, their dense inner cores can withstand the tidal forces of the host and survive as distinct, gravitationally bound substructures \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. The outer layers of the infalling halo are preferentially stripped away and absorbed into the ambient host, but the tightly bound core persists as a subhalo. As a result, $\Lambda$CDM predicts that Milky Way-mass halos are populated by a large number of subhalos spanning a wide range of masses.

The quantitative characterization of this subhalo population has been one of the major achievements of cosmological $N$-body simulations. Shortly before the turn of the century, numerical resolution reached the level necessary to robustly demonstrate the survival of subhalos within their host halos \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. Since then, dedicated high-resolution simulations have mapped the substructure of Milky Way-like galaxies in detail. Two benchmark projects are the Via Lactea II (VL-II) simulation \cite{Diemand:2008in}, which resolves subhalos down to approximately $10^4\,M_\odot$, and the Aquarius project \cite{Springel:2008cc}, which achieves comparable resolution and has been widely used to calibrate subhalo abundance predictions.

These simulations reveal a subhalo mass function that rises steeply toward lower masses, well described by a power law $dN/dM \propto M^{\alpha}$ with $\alpha \simeq -1.9$ \cite{2017ARA&A..55..343B}. A consequence of this steep slope is that the total dark matter mass per logarithmic mass decade, $M \cdot dN / d\ln M$, is approximately independent of mass \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}: comparable amounts of mass reside in subhalos at each decade of the mass scale. The theoretical mass function extends far below the resolution floor of current galactic-scale simulations. For a canonical WIMP, the smallest gravitationally bound structures --- the so-called microhalos --- could have masses comparable to that of the Earth, $\sim 10^{-6}\,M_\odot$ \cite{Cirelli:2024ssz}. To bridge this gap, repopulation techniques have been employed: Aguirre-Santaella et al. \cite{2024MNRAS.530.2496A} extended the VL-II catalog by nearly five orders of magnitude down to $\sim 10^{-1}\,M_\odot$ using Monte Carlo realizations of Milky Way-like subhalo populations. The total number of subhalos predicted by such extrapolations is vast, potentially exceeding $10^{15}$ across all mass scales.

It is worth emphasizing that these predictions are based on dark-matter-only simulations, which do not account for the gravitational influence of baryonic structures such as the Galactic disk. The impact of baryons on subhalo survival, and the observational distinction between subhalos that host visible galaxies and those that remain truly dark, are addressed in the following section.
