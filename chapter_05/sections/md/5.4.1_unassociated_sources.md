### Unassociated Sources in Fermi-LAT Catalogs
\label{sec:5.4.1}

The Fermi-LAT source catalogs provide the observational foundation for any search for dark matter subhalos among gamma-ray point sources. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:2.3}, the latest incremental release, the 4FGL-DR4 catalog \cite{2022ApJS..260...53A, 2023arXiv230712546B}, contains approximately 7,200 sources detected above a significance threshold of $4\sigma$. Of these, 2,428 sources --- roughly one third --- remain unassociated, meaning that no plausible counterpart has been identified at other wavelengths \cite{2023arXiv230712546B}. Among the unassociated population, 1,282 sources lie at Galactic latitudes $|b| > 10°$, where contamination from diffuse Galactic emission is reduced and the search for exotic source populations becomes more tractable.

The distinction between *association* and *identification* carries specific meanings in the Fermi-LAT context. An association is a statistical link between a gamma-ray detection and a candidate counterpart, established primarily through spatial coincidence using Bayesian or likelihood-ratio methods \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Because associations rely on statistical inference, they carry a known rate of false matches --- typically around 2\% for the Bayesian method and up to 10\% for likelihood-ratio associations \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Firm identification, by contrast, requires direct observational evidence: periodic variability such as pulsations, correlated variability at other wavelengths, or spatial morphology matching an extended source in another band \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Only 358 of the 4FGL sources satisfy these stricter criteria.

The completeness of the association procedure depends on several observational factors. Brighter sources are far easier to associate: essentially all sources with a test statistic $\mathrm{TS} > 500$ have known counterparts \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Fainter sources, on the other hand, suffer from larger positional uncertainties that expand the sky region to be searched and dilute the statistical significance of any spatial coincidence. Galactic latitude also plays a strong role. The association fraction drops from approximately 85\% at high latitudes to around 40\% near the Galactic plane \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}, both because dense Galactic source fields make confident associations difficult and because X-ray absorption through the disk impedes the detection of background extragalactic counterparts.

The majority of unassociated sources are expected to be ordinary astrophysical objects whose association has failed for instrumental or geometric reasons. Their spectral properties provide useful clues to their nature. At high latitudes, the average photon index of unassociated sources ($\Gamma \approx 2.24$) matches that of blazars of unknown type, suggesting that faint active galactic nuclei (AGNs) dominate this population \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Closer to the Galactic plane, the spectra soften ($\Gamma \approx 2.42$), consistent with the expected emission of young pulsars \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A}. Pulsars with unfavorable beaming geometry, which prevents detection of their pulsed emission, and blazars too faint for multi-wavelength follow-up are the most commonly cited explanations for the unassociated population (see also Section~\ref{sec:2.2}).

To date, no unassociated Fermi-LAT source has been confirmed as a dark matter subhalo \cite{2020ApJS..247...33A, 2022ApJS..260...53A}. This outcome is not unexpected. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:5.3}, even under optimistic assumptions about the annihilation cross section and J-factor distributions, the most detectable subhalos would produce gamma-ray spectra similar to those of pulsars or soft-spectrum blazars, making individual identification on spectral grounds alone extremely difficult. Nevertheless, the large unassociated population has motivated a sustained effort to search for DM subhalo candidates within it.
